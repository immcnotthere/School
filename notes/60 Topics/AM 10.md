- Invertible Matrix Theorem
	- **Invertibility:** The matrix $A$ is invertible, meaning an inverse $A^{-1}$ exists. 🔄
	- **Row Reduction:** The Reduced Row Echelon Form (RREF) of $A$ is the identity matrix $I_n$. 🔲
	- **Pivots:** The matrix has $n$ pivot positions (one in every row and column). 📍
	- **Trivial Solution:** The homogeneous equation $Ax = 0$ has _only_ the trivial solution $x = 0$. 0️⃣
	- **Linear Independence:** The columns of $A$ form a linearly independent set. 🚩
	- **Span:** The columns of $A$ span the entire space $\mathbb{R}^n$. 🌐
	- **Unique Mapping:** The linear transformation $x \mapsto Ax$ is "one-to-one" and "onto." 🎯
	- **Solvability:** The equation $Ax = b$ has at least one solution for every $b$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$. ✅

### **1. Standard Matrices ($A_T$): The "Naked Rule"**

The primary goal of the lecture is to move from knowing how a transformation acts on a specific **[[basis]]** to knowing how it acts on the **entire world** .
- **The Problem**: Transformations are often defined by a limited set of "destinations" ($T\vec{u}_1, T\vec{u}_2, \dots$) for a custom [[basis]] $B$.
- **The Translation ($A^{-1}$)**: Since the machine only "speaks" [[Basis]] $B$, we use the inverse of the input matrix to translate standard vectors into **B-coordinates** (the weights).
- **The "Naked Rule" Reveal ($A_T = C \cdot A^{-1}$)**: By multiplying the matrix of **Outputs ($C$)** by the **Inverse of the Inputs ($A^{-1}$)**, you create a single matrix that performs the translation and transformation in one step.
- **Final Utility**: This allows you to find the image of **any** vector $\vec{x}$ by simply calculating $A_T \cdot \vec{x}$ in standard coordinates .

---

### **2. Rotations ($R_\theta$): The Invertible "Spin"**

Rotations are a specific type of linear transformation in $\mathbb{R}^2$ that change a vector's direction while preserving its length .
- **The Columns as Landing Spots**: To build the rotation matrix ($A_\theta$), we track where the standard [[basis]] vectors land: $\vec{e}_1$ lands at $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ and $\vec{e}_2$ lands at $(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$ .
- **The Rule**: $A_\theta = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$.
- **Property of Invertibility**: Rotations are "rigid" transformations. You can always "spin back" using $-\theta$, meaning no data is lost during the process .
---

### **3. Projections ($\Pi_m$): The Selective Filter**

Orthogonal projection is the process of mapping a vector to its "shadow" on a subspace, such as a line $y=mx$ .
- **Condition 1 ($P\vec{x} = \vec{x}$)**: If a vector already lies on the target line, the rule does nothing to it; it is already "in" that world .
- **Condition 2 ($P\vec{x} = 0$)**: If a vector is at a $90^\circ$ angle (orthogonal) to the line, the rule deletes it entirely .
- **Condition 3 (Shadow vs. Error)**: For any other vector, the rule keeps the component pointing along the line (the shadow) and "trashes" the part pointing away (the error) .
- **Non-Invertibility**: Because projection "squashes" 2D data into a 1D line, it is **irreversible**—you cannot reconstruct the original 2D vector from its 1D shadow .
---

### **4. The 3-Step Strategy: "Building Complex Rules"**

To find the matrix for a projection onto a "tilted" line ($y=mx$), the lecture uses a composition of three simpler transformations . This strategy allows you to solve a hard problem by temporarily moving it into an easier "world" .
- **Step 1: Rotate Clockwise ($R_{-\theta}$)**: Rotate the entire space by $-\theta$ (where $\theta = \tan^{-1}(m)$) to make the tilted line lie flat on the x-axis .
- **Step 2: Project onto X-axis ($\Pi_0$)**: Apply the simple projection rule $[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}]$ to squash the vector onto the now-flat line .
- **Step 3: Rotate Counter-Clockwise ($R_{\theta}$)**: Rotate the world back to its original orientation so the line is tilted again .
- **The Master Matrix**: Multiplying these three matrices in reverse order ($R_{\theta} \cdot \Pi_0 \cdot R_{-\theta}$) results in the final projection matrix formula: $\begin{bmatrix} \cos^2\theta & \cos\theta\sin\theta \\ \cos\theta\sin\theta & \sin^2\theta \end{bmatrix}$.

### **5. Idempotency and Composition**

These two properties define how transformations interact with themselves and each other.
- **Idempotency ($P^2 = P$)**: Once a vector is projected, applying the rule again does nothing because the vector is already on the line .
- **Composition ($S \circ T$)**: Performing multiple actions is equivalent to multiplying their matrices . Note that the rule that happens **first** must be on the **right** in the matrix product .
- **Linearity:** For a rule to be valid in this system, it must respect **addition** (transforming the sum is the same as adding the transformed parts) and **scaling** (stretching the input stretches the output).

## **1. Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors: The "Characteristic" Scaling**

These concepts describe special directions where a matrix doesn't rotate a vector, but only stretches or squashes it.
- **Eigenvalue ($\lambda$):** A scalar that represents the factor by which a specific vector is scaled during a transformation.

- **Eigenvector ($\vec{x}$):** A non-zero vector that, when multiplied by a matrix, results in a simple scaling of itself ($A\vec{x} = \lambda\vec{x}$).

- **The Characteristic Equation:** To find these special values, you solve for when the matrix $(\lambda I - A)$ is **not invertible**.

---

## **2. Determinants: The "Invertibility Gauge"**

The determinant is a single number calculated from a square matrix that determines if the "instructions" are healthy or broken.
- **The Invertibility Test:** A matrix is invertible **if and only if** its determinant is not zero ($det(A) \neq 0$).

- **Geometric Meaning:** In 2D, the determinant represents the area scaling factor of the transformation.
- **Basic Formulas:**
    - **$2 \times 2$ Case:** For $[\begin{smallmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{smallmatrix}]$, the determinant is $ad - bc$.
    - **$3 \times 3$ Case:** For $[\begin{smallmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & i \end{smallmatrix}]$, it is $aei + bfg + cdh - ceg - bdi - afh$.
---

## **3. Algebraic Properties of Determinants**

Determinants follow strict rules when you combine matrices or flip their orientation.
- **Multiplicative Property:** The determinant of a product is the product of the determinants ($det(AB) = det(A)det(B)$).
- **Transpose Equality:** Swapping rows and columns does not change the determinant ($det(A^T) = det(A)$).
- **Inversion Rule:** The determinant of an inverse matrix is the reciprocal of the original ($det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{det(A)}$).
---

## **4. Interaction with Elementary Row Operations (EROs)**

How you manipulate a matrix changes the determinant in predictable ways.

- **Row Swaps:** Swapping two rows multiplies the determinant by $-1$.
- **Row Scaling:** Multiplying a row by a scalar $\alpha$ multiplies the entire determinant by $\alpha$.
- **Row Addition:** Adding a multiple of one row to another **does not change** the determinant at all.

---

## **5. Cramer's Rule: Solving via Determinants**

This is a specific method for solving systems of linear equations using ratios of determinants.
- **The Procedure:** Each variable in a system ($x, y, \dots$) can be found by replacing one column of the matrix with the target vector and dividing that new determinant by the original $det(A)$.
- **Constraint:** This only works if the original matrix is invertible ($det(A) \neq 0$).
---

## **6. The 3x3 "Sarrus" Shortcut**

A specific visual method for calculating $3 \times 3$ determinants by appending the first two columns to the end of the matrix.
- **The Method:** Multiply along the three main diagonals and add them; then multiply along the three "back" diagonals and subtract them.
- **Critical Warning:** This specific shortcut **does not generalize** to $4 \times 4$ or higher matrices.

## **1. General Definition: Cofactor Expansion**

For any $n \times n$ matrix, the determinant can be calculated recursively by breaking it down into smaller $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ matrices.

+1

- **Submatrix ($A_{ij}$):** The $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ matrix obtained by deleting row $i$ and column $j$ from matrix $A$.
    
- **Minor ($|A_{ij}|$):** The determinant of the submatrix $A_{ij}$.
    
- **Cofactor ($C_{ij}$):** The signed minor, defined as $C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} \cdot |A_{ij}|$.
    
- **The Summation Formula:** You can find $|A|$ by "developing along" any single row ($i$) or column ($j$) :
    
    +1
    
    - **Along Row $i$:** $\det A = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik} C_{ik}$.
        
    - **Along Column $j$:** $\det A = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{kj} C_{kj}$.

    +2

## **3. Elementary Row Operations (EROs) and Determinants**

Using Gauss-Jordan steps is the most efficient way to calculate large determinants. Each operation impacts the value in a specific way:

+1

- **Row Scaling ($\alpha R_i \to R_i$):** Multiplying a row by a scalar $\alpha$ multiplies the determinant by $\alpha$.
    
    +1
    
    - **The Proof:** Since $C_{ij}$ does not include row $i$, scaling row $i$ by $\alpha$ results in $|B| [cite_start]= \sum_{j=1}^{n} \alpha a_{ij} C_{ij} = \alpha |A|$ .
        
- **Row Swap ($R_i \leftrightarrow R_j$):** Swapping any two rows multiplies the determinant by $-1$.
    
    +1
    
    - **Adjacent Swaps:** Swapping row $i$ and $i+1$ changes the sign because the power in $(-1)^{i+j}$ shifts by one.
        
        +1
        
    - **General Swaps:** To swap rows $k$ distance apart, it takes $2k-1$ adjacent swaps. Since $2k-1$ is always odd, the sign always flips once: $|B| = (-1)^{odd} \cdot |A| [cite_start]= -|A|$.
        
        +3
        
- **Row Replacement ($R_i + \alpha R_k \to R_i$):** Adding a multiple of one row to another **does not change** the determinant.
    
    +1
    
    - **The Logic:** This operation effectively splits the determinant into the original $|A|$ plus a temporary matrix with two identical rows. Any matrix with two identical rows has a determinant of $0$ .
        
        +4

## **4. The Efficiency Algorithm**

Because calculating an $n \times n$ determinant via cofactor expansion requires $n!$ multiplications, the lecture proposes a faster computational approach :

1. **Transform** matrix $A$ into an upper triangular form using only **swaps** and **replacements** .
    
2. **Count** the total number of row swaps performed, called $\sigma$ .
    
3. **Apply the formula:**
    
    $$|A| = (-1)^\sigma \cdot (a_{11} \cdot a_{22} \cdot \dots \cdot a_{nn})$$
    
    (where $a_{ii}$ are the diagonal entries of the resulting triangular matrix) .

The lecture establishes that the determinant of a product is the product of the individual determinants. This is proven by observing how elementary matrices ($E$) affect any matrix $A$ :

+1

- **Elementary Matrix Determinants:**
    
    - **Scaling ($\alpha R_i \to R_i$):** $|E| [cite_start]= \alpha$.
        
    - **Row Swap ($R_i \leftrightarrow R_j$):** $|E| [cite_start]= -1$.
        
    - **Row Replacement ($R_i + \alpha R_k \to R_i$):** $|E| [cite_start]= 1$.
        
- **The Multiplicative Rule:** $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$.
    
    - If $A$ is invertible, it can be written as a product of elementary matrices ($A = E_L \dots E_k$), making the proof straightforward .
        
    - If $A$ is not invertible, $\det(A) = 0$, and $\det(AB)$ will also be $0$ .
        
- **Transpose Rule:** $\det(A^T) = \det(A)$, proven by expanding along the first column of $A^T$ versus the first row of $A$ .
    
    +1

### **2. The Adjugate Matrix and Inverses**

The lecture introduces a way to find the inverse of a matrix without using Gaussian elimination.

- **Cofactor Matrix:** A matrix where every entry $a_{ij}$ is replaced by its cofactor $C_{ij}$.
    
    +1
    
- **Adjugate ($adj(A)$):** The **transpose** of the cofactor matrix .
    
- **The Inverse Formula:** If $A$ is invertible ($\det A \neq 0$), then:
    
    $$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{|A|} \cdot adj(A)$$
    
- **Key Property:** $A \cdot adj(A) = \det(A) \cdot I$.
    
    +1

    $$x_j = \frac{\det(A_j(\vec{b}))}{\det(A)}$$
    
- **$A_j(\vec{b})$:** The matrix created by replacing the **j-th column** of $A$ with the vector $\vec{b}$.

### **4. Eigenvalues and the Characteristic Polynomial**

The lecture returns to eigenvalues, defining them as the roots of a specific polynomial .

- **Characteristic Equation:** $\det(\lambda I - A) = 0$.
    
    +1
    
- **Characteristic Polynomial ($P_A(\lambda)$):** A polynomial of degree $n$ resulting from the expansion of $\det(\lambda I - A)$ .
    
- **Trace ($Tr(A)$):** The sum of the diagonal entries ($\sum a_{ii}$) .
    
- **Polynomial Structure:** For an $n \times n$ matrix, the polynomial looks like:
    
    $$P_A(\lambda) = \lambda^n - Tr(A)\lambda^{n-1} + \dots + (-1)^n|A|$$
---
To find the **eigenvalues** of a matrix, we follow a specific workflow: we build the "scaling" version of the matrix, find its determinant to create a polynomial, and solve for the roots of that polynomial .

Here is the walkthrough of the example from **Slide 29**:

### **1. Set up the Matrix**

We are given the $3 \times 3$ matrix $B$:

$$B = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 4 & 1 \\ -2 & -6 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$$

To find the eigenvalues, we must solve the **Characteristic Equation**, which is $\det(\lambda I - B) = 0$ .

+1

$$\lambda I - B = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda-2 & -3 & -1 \\ -1 & \lambda-4 & -1 \\ 2 & 6 & \lambda+1 \end{bmatrix}$$

---

### **2. Calculate the Characteristic Polynomial ($P_B(\lambda)$)**

Expanding the determinant (using the $3 \times 3$ shortcut or cofactor expansion) gives the following expression :

$$P_B(\lambda) = (\lambda-2)(\lambda-4)(\lambda+1) + 6 + 6 - [-2(\lambda-4) - 6(\lambda-2) - 3(\lambda+1)]$$

After expanding the terms and combining like variables, the lecture simplifies this to a cubic polynomial:

+1

$$P_B(\lambda) = \lambda^3 - 5\lambda^2 + 7\lambda - 3$$

---

### **3. Find the Roots (The Spectrum)**

To find the actual eigenvalues, we set the polynomial to zero: $\lambda^3 - 5\lambda^2 + 7\lambda - 3 = 0$.

- **Test for Rational Roots:** We try potential roots based on the factors of the constant term ($-3$), which are $\pm1$ and $\pm3$ .
    
- **Testing $\lambda = 1$:** $1^3 - 5(1)^2 + 7(1) - 3 = 1 - 5 + 7 - 3 = 0$. This works!.
    
- **Testing $\lambda = 3$:** $3^3 - 5(3)^2 + 7(3) - 3 = 27 - 45 + 21 - 3 = 0$. This also works!.

### **4. Factoring and Multiplicity**

Knowing that $(\lambda-1)$ and $(\lambda-3)$ are factors, we can write the polynomial as :

$$P_B(\lambda) = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 3)(\lambda - \lambda_3)$$

By comparing the constant terms or performing division, the lecture reveals the final root is another $1$:

+1

$$P_B(\lambda) = (\lambda - 1)^2(\lambda - 3)$$

**Conclusion:** The **Spectrum** (set of eigenvalues) of matrix $B$ is $\{1, 3\}$.

+1

- **$\lambda = 1$** has a multiplicity of 2 (it is a repeated root) .
    
- **$\lambda = 3$** has a multiplicity of 1

## **1. Eigenvectors and Eigenspaces**

While eigenvalues ($\lambda$) provide the "scaling factor," eigenvectors represent the specific "directions" being scaled .

- **Finding Eigenvectors**: For a given $\lambda$, we find eigenvectors by solving the homogeneous linear system: $(\lambda I - A)\vec{x} = \vec{0}$ .
    
- **The Eigenspace ($E_\lambda$)**: This is the set of all eigenvectors associated with $\lambda$, plus the zero vector .
    
- **Formal Identity**: $E_{\lambda} = \text{null}(\lambda I - A)$, which makes it a formal subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$ .
    
- **Invariance**: $E_\lambda$ is an **invariant subspace**, meaning any vector within it stays within that subspace after being transformed by $A$ .
    

---

## **2. Algebraic vs. Geometric Multiplicity**

The "weight" or "strength" of an eigenvalue is measured in two distinct ways:

- **Algebraic Multiplicity**: The number of times a specific eigenvalue $\lambda_0$ appears as a root of the characteristic polynomial $P_A(\lambda)$ .
    
- **Geometric Multiplicity**: The number of linearly independent eigenvectors associated with $\lambda_0$ .
    
    - This value is exactly equal to the **dimension** of the eigenspace $E_{\lambda_0}$.
        
- **The Fundamental Inequality**: For any eigenvalue, the geometric multiplicity is **always less than or equal to** the algebraic multiplicity .

## **4. Stochastic Matrices and Stability**

Spectral theory is used to find the "steady state" in systems governed by probability.

- **Probability Vector**: A vector where all entries are $\ge 0$ and their sum equals 1 .
    
- **Left Stochastic Matrix**: A matrix where every column is a probability vector .
    
- **The Unity Fact**: If a matrix $M$ is stochastic, then **$\lambda = 1$ is always an eigenvalue** .
    
- **Steady State Persistence**: If $M$ is stochastic and $\vec{p}$ is a probability vector, then the product $M\vec{p}$ is also a probability vector .

## **5. Diagonalization: The Decomposition Goal**

The ultimate goal of spectral theory is to simplify a matrix by breaking it into its constituent parts .

+1

- **The Formula**: $A = UDU^{-1}$.
    
    - **Matrix D**: A diagonal matrix containing the eigenvalues .
        
    - **Matrix U**: A matrix where the columns are the corresponding eigenvectors .
        
        +1
        
- **Matrix Powers**: This allows for the rapid calculation of $A^n = UD^nU^{-1}$ .
    
    - As $n \to \infty$, components with eigenvalues $|\lambda| [cite_start]< 1$ vanish, leaving only the "Steady State" $(\lambda=1)$ .
---
Diagonalization is the process of finding a "change of [[basis]]" that turns a complicated transformation into a simple, diagonal set of scaling instructions.

+1

#### **1. Identify the Spectrum (Eigenvalues)**

For the stochastic matrix $m$:

$$m = \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 & 0.5 & 0.6 \\ 0.2 & 0.5 & 0.2 \\ 0.7 & 0 & 0.2 \end{bmatrix}$$

The characteristic polynomial $P_m(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - m)$ reveals the eigenvalues :

- **$\lambda_1 = 1$** (The Steady State) .
    
    +2
    
- **$\lambda_2 = 0.3$** (A decaying component) .
    
    +2
    
- **$\lambda_3 = -0.5$** (An oscillating/decaying component) .
    
    +1
    

#### **2. Find the [[Basis]] of Eigenvectors**

We solve $(\lambda I - m)\vec{x} = \vec{0}$ for each eigenvalue to find the directions of scaling :

- **For $\lambda_1 = 1$**: The system reduces to $x = \frac{8}{7}z$ and $y = \frac{6}{7}z$. Choosing $z=7$ gives the eigenvector **$\vec{x}_1 = [8, 6, 7]^T$** .
    
- **For $\lambda_2 = 0.3$**: The system yields the eigenvector **$\vec{x}_2 = [1, -1, 7]^T$** .
    
- **For $\lambda_3 = -0.5$**: The system yields the eigenvector **$\vec{x}_3 = [-1, 0, 1]^T$** .
    

These three vectors are linearly independent and form a [[basis]] for $\mathbb{R}^3$.

#### **3. Construct the Component Matrices ($U$ and $D$)**

Now we "package" these results into two matrices :

- **Matrix $D$ (The "Rule" Matrix):** A diagonal matrix containing the eigenvalues in order .
    
    $$D = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -0.5 \end{bmatrix}$$
    
- **Matrix $U$ (The "Translator" Matrix):** A matrix where the columns are the corresponding eigenvectors .
    
    +1
    
    $$U = \begin{bmatrix} 8/21 & 1 & -1 \\ 6/21 & -1 & 0 \\ 7/21 & 7 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
    

#### **4. The Final Assembly ($m = UDU^{-1}$)**

The original matrix is now decomposed into $m = UDU^{-1}$.

- **Utility:** To calculate $m^n$, you only need to calculate $D^n$ .
    
- **Long-term Behavior ($n \to \infty$):** * $(0.3)^n \to 0$.
    
    +1
    
    - $(-0.5)^n \to 0$.
        
        +1
        
    - The only part that survives is the $\lambda = 1$ component, meaning the system eventually settles into the **Steady State** defined by $\vec{x}_1$ .
        
        +1

### **1. Markov Chains: Systems in Motion**

A Markov Chain is a sequence of probability vectors that describe the state of a system at different time intervals ($k=0, 1, 2, \dots$) .

+1

- **The Components:**
    
    - **States ($S_n$):** A system exists in one of $n$ possible states .
        
    - **Initial Probability Vector ($\vec{p}_0$):** A vector where each entry represents the probability of starting in a specific state .
        
    - **Transition Matrix ($m$):** A stochastic matrix where the entry $m_{ij}$ is the probability of moving from state $i$ to state $j$ in one time period .
        
        +1
        
- **The Dynamics:**
    
    - The state after $k$ steps is calculated as $\vec{p}_k = m^k \cdot \vec{p}_0$ .
        
    - The transition probabilities remain constant in each period.
        

---

### **2. The Steady State Vector**

A critical question in Markov Chains is what happens as time goes to infinity ($k \to \infty$).

+1

- **Regular Stochastic Matrix:** A matrix is regular if there is some power $k$ such that all entries of $m^k$ are strictly positive ($>0$) . This means any state can eventually reach any other state .
    
    +1
    
- **Steady State ($\vec{\pi}_0$):** For a regular stochastic matrix, there exists a unique probability vector such that $m\vec{\pi}_0 = \vec{\pi}_0$ .
    
- **Convergence:** Regardless of the starting distribution $\vec{p}_0$, the sequence $m^k\vec{p}_0$ will eventually converge to this steady state vector $\vec{\pi}_0$ .
    
    +2
    

---

### **3. Diagonalizability and Similarity**

Diagonalization is the mathematical shortcut for calculating high powers of matrices like $m^k$ .

+1

- **Similar Matrices:** Matrix $A$ and $B$ are similar if there exists an invertible matrix $U$ such that $B = UAU^{-1}$ .
    
- **Diagonalizable:** A matrix is diagonalizable if it is similar to a diagonal matrix $D$ .
    
- **Power Rule:** If $m = UDU^{-1}$, then $m^k = UD^kU^{-1}$. Because $D$ is diagonal, $D^k$ is simply each diagonal entry (eigenvalue) raised to the power $k$.
    
    +3

### **1. Similarity of Matrices**

Two matrices are considered "similar" ($A \sim B$) if they represent the same linear transformation but under different coordinate systems.

+1

- **Formal Definition:** $A$ and $B$ are similar if there exists an invertible matrix $P$ such that $B = P^{-1}AP$ .
    
- **Similarity as an Equivalence Relation:** Similarity follows three mathematical properties:
    
    - **Reflexive:** $A \sim A$ because $I^{-1}AI = A$ .
        
    - **Symmetric:** If $A \sim B$, then $B \sim A$ .
        
    - **Transitive:** If $A \sim B$ and $B \sim C$, then $A \sim C$ .
        
- **Shared Properties:** Similar matrices share the same **characteristic polynomial** and therefore the same **eigenvalues** .

### **2. Diagonalizability**

A matrix $A$ is **diagonalizable** if it is similar to a diagonal matrix $D$ ($A = PDP^{-1}$) .

+1

- **The Diagonalization Criterion:** An $n \times n$ matrix is diagonalizable if and only if it has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors . These eigenvectors form a **[[basis]]** for $\mathbb{R}^n$.
    
    +1
    
- **Alternative Requirements:**
    
    - All eigenvalues must be real .
        
    - For every eigenvalue, the **geometric multiplicity** (dimension of the eigenspace) must equal the **algebraic multiplicity** .
        
- **Independence of Eigenvectors:** Eigenvectors corresponding to _distinct_ eigenvalues are always linearly independent . This is proven via **induction** .
    
    +1

### **Proof: The Predator-Prey Survival Ratio (Lecture 18)**

The lecture uses **Cramer's Rule** to determine exactly what initial conditions are needed to ensure the long-term survival of both owls and mice.

**The Setup:**

The population at any time $k$ is determined by the growth component $c_1$ associated with the eigenvalue $\lambda = 1.2$.

$$\begin{bmatrix} m_k \\ o_k \end{bmatrix} \approx c_1 \cdot (1.2)^k \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

For the population to survive, $c_1$ **must be greater than zero** ($c_1 > 0$).

+1

**The Calculation:**

We find $c_1$ by solving the system relating the initial population to the eigenvector [[basis]]:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} m_0 \\ o_0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Using **Cramer's Rule** for $c_1$:

$$c_1 = \frac{\det \begin{bmatrix} m_0 & 2 \\ o_0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}}{\det \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix}} = \frac{5m_0 - 2o_0}{8}$$

**The Conclusion:**

For $c_1 > 0$, the numerator must be positive:

- $5m_0 - 2o_0 > 0$
    
- $5m_0 > 2o_0$
    
- **$m_0/o_0 > 2/5$**
    

This means there must be more than **0.4 mice for every owl** at the start, or the entire ecosystem will eventually collapse.
