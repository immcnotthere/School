**The question the lecture answers:** how does a country's account with the rest of the world add up, and how does that tie to national saving, investment and the exchange rate system?

## 1. Balance of payments, current account, capital account
> 1. Balance of payments (BOP): the value of the transactions between a country's residents, businesses, and government with the rest of the world for a specific period of time.
>
> 2. Current account: includes goods and services transactions, transactions associated with income flows from the ownership of foreign assets, and unilateral transfers of money between countries.
>
> 3. Capital account (also called financial account): the purchases and sales of foreign assets by domestic residents as well as the purchases and sales of domestic assets by foreign residents.
>
> Net foreign asset (NFA): the value of the assets that country owns abroad, minus the value of the domestic assets owned by foreigners.
>
> International reserves: the assets of the central bank that are not denominated in the domestic currency, such as gold and assets denominated in foreign currency.

**Claude's explanation (not in the handout):**
- Current account = what the country **earns and pays** abroad: exports minus imports, investment income, transfers.
- Capital account = what the country **buys and sells** abroad in assets: stocks, bonds, property, bank deposits.
- Net foreign asset position, in symbols: $\text{NFA} = \text{assets owned abroad} - \text{domestic assets owned by foreigners}$. A positive NFA means the country is a net creditor to the world.

## 2. Double-entry accounting
> 4. Double-entry accounting system: each transaction gives rise to a credit (inflows) and a debit (outflows), both of equal value.

> **Exhibit 4.1** Summary of the Accounts of the Balance of Payments

| Debits (recorded with a −) | Credits (recorded with a +) |
| --- | --- |
| **I. CURRENT ACCOUNT** | |
| (A) TRADE BALANCE (Transactions in goods, services, and transfers) | |
| Imports to the United States | Exports from the United States |
| (B) INVESTMENT INCOME ACCOUNT | |
| Payment by the United States of dividends and interest to foreigners | Receipt by the United States of dividends and interest from foreigners |
| **II. CAPITAL ACCOUNT** | |
| Capital Outflows | Capital Inflows |
| Increase in U.S. ownership of foreign assets | Increase in foreign ownership of U.S. assets |
| Decrease of foreign ownership of U.S. assets | Decrease in U.S. ownership of foreign assets |
| **III. OFFICIAL RESERVES ACCOUNT** | |
| Increase in official reserves of the U.S. central bank | Decrease in official reserves of the U.S. central bank |
| Decrease in dollar reserves of foreign central banks | Increase in dollar reserves of foreign central banks |

**Claude's explanation (not in the handout):** read each row as a rule: money **coming into** the US is a credit (+), money **going out** is a debit (−). A US export brings payment in, so it is a credit. A foreigner buying a US asset brings money in, so it is a credit under capital inflows.

## 3. What happens to the BOP of the US?
> What happens to the BOP of the US?
> Example 1: Dell sells \$20 million of computers to Samsung in Korea.
> Example 2: A Japanese resident purchases a condo for \$.5 M in Santa Cruz.
> Example 3: Alice gives her Dutch cousin a \$36 case of Budweiser in exchange for Heineken.

**Claude's answers (not in the handout; check against what Prof. Hu worked in class):**

| Example | Credit (+) | Debit (−) | Net |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Dell sells computers to Samsung | Exports from the US: **+\$20M** (current account) | The payment arrives as a foreign asset or foreign-held dollars falling: **−\$20M** (capital account, outflow) | 0 |
| 2. Japanese resident buys a Santa Cruz condo | Foreign ownership of a US asset rises: **+\$0.5M** (capital inflow) | The payment takes some form: e.g. the seller receives a foreign deposit, so US ownership of foreign assets rises: **−\$0.5M** (capital outflow) | 0 |
| 3. Budweiser for Heineken | Exports: **+\$36** (current account) | Imports: **−\$36** (current account) | 0 |

- In Examples 1 and 2 the debit's exact line depends on **how the payment is made**; the handout does not say. In every case the two entries are equal and opposite, which is the double-entry rule.
- Example 3 is barter: both entries land in the **current account**, so it changes neither the current account total nor the capital account.

## 4. The BOP identity
> **BOP identity**: current account + capital account = 0

$$\text{Current account} + \text{Capital account} = 0$$

**Claude's explanation (not in the handout):**
- Every transaction has two equal entries, so the sum over all of them is zero: a current account **surplus** must be matched by a capital account **deficit** (the country lends the difference to the world), and the reverse.
- Exhibit 4.1 prints official reserves as a third block. The handout does not say which side the identity counts reserves on; check the textbook, chapter 4.

## 5. National income and product accounts (NIPA)
> 5. National income and product accounts (NIPA)
>
> Gross national income (GNI): the flow of resources over a period of time that allows residents of the country to consume during that period and to provide for their future consumption through saving and the accumulation of wealth.
>
> Net foreign income (NFI): net factor income from abroad plus net unilateral transfers from abroad

> $$\text{GNI} = \text{GDP} + \text{NFI}$$
>
> $$\text{GNI} - (C + I + G) = \text{CA}$$
>
> $$S = \text{GNI} - C - G$$
>
> $$S - I = \text{CA}$$

**Claude's derivation, line by line (not in the handout):**
1. Start: $\text{GNI} - (C + I + G) = \text{CA}$. What the country earns minus what it spends (consumption, investment, government) is the current account.
2. Saving is what is left of income after consumption and government spending: $S = \text{GNI} - C - G$.
3. Rewrite line 1 as $(\text{GNI} - C - G) - I = \text{CA}$.
4. Substitute line 2: $S - I = \text{CA}$.

**Reading it:** a country that saves more than it invests has a current account surplus, so it lends the surplus to the world (a capital outflow). A country that invests more than it saves runs a deficit and borrows from abroad.

## 6. Exchange rate systems around the world
> 6. Exchange rate systems around the world
>
> Floating currencies – determined by the market forces of supply and demand (i.e., U.S., Japan, European Union, Australia, and Sweden)
>
> Managed floating – countries whose Central Banks intervene enough that the IMF can't classify them as freely floating (i.e., Argentina, Brazil, Columbia, Indonesia, Israel, Mexico and South Africa)
>
> Fixed/pegged currencies – "pegging" a currency to another or a basket of currencies (i.e., China)
>
> No separate legal tender – adopt a foreign currency (i.e., Ecuador, El Salvador and Panama have adopted the U.S. dollar)
>
> Target zone – exchange rate is kept within band (e.g. Cyprus)
>
> Crawling pegs – changes are kept lower than preset limits that are adjusted regularly w/ inflation

**Claude's explanation (not in the handout):** the list runs from **least** central bank control (floating) to **most** (no separate legal tender, where the country has no currency of its own to manage). Your Lecture 2 notes use *appreciate/depreciate* for floating currencies and *revalue/devalue* for currencies a government sets; see [[Exchange Rate Regimes (Peg and Managed Float)]].

## 7. Central banks' balance sheet
> 7. Central banks' balance sheet

| Assets | Liabilites |
| --- | --- |
| Official international reserves | Deposits of private financial institutions (Bank reserves) |
| Domestic credit | Currency in circulation |
| • Government bonds | Other |
| • Loans to domestic financial institutions | |
| Other | |

**Claude's explanation (not in the handout):** a central bank's assets are what it holds (foreign reserves, government bonds, loans to banks). Its liabilities are the money it has created: cash in circulation and the deposits banks keep with it. Buying or selling an asset moves the matching liability, which is why the next section matters.

## 8. Foreign exchange interventions
> 8. Foreign exchange interventions: non-sterilized and sterilized

**Claude's explanation (not in the handout; check against class):**
- **Non-sterilized intervention:** the central bank buys (or sells) foreign currency and lets its own money supply change. To defend a weak currency it **sells** foreign reserves and takes domestic currency out of circulation: reserves (asset) fall and bank reserves or currency (liability) fall by the same amount.
- **Sterilized intervention:** it also offsets the domestic effect, for example by buying or selling government bonds, so the money supply stays unchanged. On the balance sheet, a fall in one asset (reserves) is matched by a rise in another (domestic credit).
