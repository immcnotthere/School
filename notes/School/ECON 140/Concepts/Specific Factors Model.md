## Slide 2 — Introduction to the Specific Factors Model

### Key Points

- The [[Ricardian Model]] features only **one factor of production** ([[labor]]) → ignores [[distributional effects of trade]]
- It emphasizes [[Comparative Advantage]] arising from cross-country differences in **technology**, but ignores differences in **[[factor endowments]]**
- Today: studying **cross-country differences in multiple [[factor endowments]]**
  - [[Capital]] (K), [[Land]] (T), [[Labor]] (L)
  - [[Winners and losers from trade]]

## Slide 3 — The Specific Factors Model: Assumptions

### Setup

Two goods: **Food (F)** and **Clothing (C)**

$$Q_C = Q_C(K, L_C) \qquad Q_F = Q_F(T, L_F)$$

### Assumptions

| Factor | Mobility | Type |
|--------|----------|------|
| [[Capital]] (K) | Fixed to Clothing sector | [[Specific Factor]] |
| [[Land]] (T) | Fixed to Food sector | [[Specific Factor]] |
| [[Labor]] (L) | Moves freely across sectors | [[Mobile Factor]] |

- [[Constant Returns to Scale]] (CRS) in production
- [[Diminishing Marginal Returns]] and [[Factor Complementarity]]
- [[Perfectly competitive markets]] — producers take prices, rental rates, and wages as given

### 📝 Notes
> We assume that local prices do not determine the global economy dynamics, we take the world price as given
>

## Slide 4 — What Are Specific Factors?

### Definition

> Certain factors are **permanently tied** to particular sectors; others are **mobile** across all sectors. The distinction is a question of **speed of adjustment**, not a sharp line.

### Real-World Evidence

- [[Worker mobility]] varies with worker characteristics (e.g. age) and occupation (general vs. job-specific skills)
- Mobility can be measured by the **duration of unemployment** following displacement
- Most U.S. workers find new employment in other sectors within **4 years**
- BUT: displaced workers re-employed in a **different occupation** suffer an average **18% permanent wage drop**

## Slide 5 — PPF in the Specific Factors Model

### Production Technology Comparison

| Model | Factors | PPF Shape |
|-------|---------|-----------|
| [[Ricardian Model]] | CRS, 1 factor (L) | Linear |
| [[Specific Factors Model]] | CRS, 3 factors (K, T, L) | Concave (bowed out) |

### CRS Reminder

$$G(\lambda K, \lambda L) = \lambda G(K, L) \quad \text{for any } \lambda > 0$$

Scaling all inputs by $\lambda$ scales output by $\lambda$.

## Slide 6 — PPF Shape: The Diagram

The [[Production Possibility Frontier]] in the [[Specific Factors Model]] is **concave** (bowed outward), unlike the linear PPF in the [[Ricardian Model]].

- X-axis: $Q_C$ (Production of Clothing)
- Y-axis: $Q_F$ (Production of Food)

> **Q: What is the slope of the PPF?** → See Slides 7–8

### 📝 Notes
> No longer in the linear world
> No longer can the PPF be a singel value for a slope
> Now it is a function of the marginal products
> 

---

## Slide 7 — Slope of the PPF (Part 1)

### Intuition: Shifting Labor Between Sectors

Suppose we shift **one unit of $L$** from Food (F) → Clothing (C):

- C sector gains one unit of L → output of C **rises** by $MPL_C$
- F sector loses one unit of L → output of F **falls** by $MPL_F$

### [[Marginal Product of Labor]]

$$\frac{\partial Q_F}{\partial L} = MPL_F \qquad \frac{\partial Q_C}{\partial L} = MPL_C$$

### 📝 Notes
> We're in an interesting case now where one unit of additional L employed in C increases unit of C by MPL_C, losing F Sector one unit of F so it falls by the mariginal product of labor of F or MPL_F

---

## Slide 8 — Slope of the PPF (Part 2)

### Slope Formula

$$\left|\frac{dQ_F}{dQ_C}\right| = \frac{MPL_F}{MPL_C}$$

### Why the PPF is Concave

As $Q_C \uparrow$ (more labor shifted to Clothing):

- More L in Clothing → $MPL_C \downarrow$ ([[Diminishing Marginal Returns]])
- Less L in Food → $MPL_F \uparrow$
- Therefore the ratio $\frac{MPL_F}{MPL_C}$ **increases** → slope steepens

$$\Rightarrow \text{[[Opportunity cost]] of Clothing (in terms of foregone Food) increases}$$

## Slide 9 — International Trade in the SF Model

### Setup

[[Small Open Economy]] (SOE) assumption:

> A **[[small open economy]]** takes [[world prices]] as given. Nothing it does unilaterally can affect world relative prices.

Assume: $\left(\frac{P_C}{P_F}\right)^A < \left(\frac{P_C}{P_F}\right)^T$

(Autarky relative price of clothing < World relative price of clothing)

### What Happens?

- The SOE can produce Clothing **more cheaply** than the world price
- → **[[Comparative Advantage]]** in Clothing
- → Economy becomes **more specialized** in Clothing
- → **Exports** Clothing, **Imports** Food

### 📝 Notes
> 

---

## Slide 10 — Trade Equilibrium: Incomplete Specialization

### Adjustment Mechanism

Since $\left(\frac{P_C}{P_F}\right)^A < \left(\frac{P_C}{P_F}\right)^T$:

- More workers shift to Clothing: $L_C \uparrow$, $L_F \downarrow$
- Clothing sector **expands**, Food sector **shrinks**

### Why [[Incomplete Specialization]]?

As $L$ leaves Food:

$$MPL_F \uparrow \Rightarrow \text{Return to } L \text{ in Food rises} \Rightarrow \text{Less incentive to switch}$$

This self-correcting mechanism prevents **complete specialization** (unlike the [[Ricardian Model]] where complete specialization is possible due to constant $MPL$).

## Slide 11 — Distributional Effects of Trade

Measured via changes in **real** wages, **real** return to [[land]], and **real** return to [[capital]].

### Winners and Losers

| Group | Outcome | Reason |
|-------|---------|--------|
| [[Specific factors]] in **export** sector (Capital K) | ✅ **Win** | Export sector expands → return to tied factor rises |
| [[Specific factors]] in **import** sector (Land T) | ❌ **Lose** | Import sector shrinks → return to tied factor falls |
| [[Mobile factor]] ([[Labor]] L) | ❓ **Ambiguous** | Real wage depends on which sector's price deflator is used |

### 📝 Notes
> By real we mean we need to normalize by price, so we get an idea of purchasing power on wages, land, and capital 
> The effect of this is that there's a trinary of Winners, Losers, And Ambiguous
>

## Slide 12 — Gains From Trade Summary

### General Principle

When a country **opens to trade** or **terms of trade improve**:

$$\text{Specific factors in export sector} \rightarrow \text{Gain}$$
$$\text{Specific factors in import sector} \rightarrow \text{Lose}$$
$$\text{Converse holds when terms of trade deteriorate}$$

This explains observed **preferences over [[trade policy]]**.

### Historical Example

- **1830s UK**: Exported manufactures, imported food
- **[[Corn Laws]]**: [[Landowners]] lobbied to maintain tariffs on food imports
  - As specific factors in the food (import-competing) sector, they stood to **lose** from free trade
