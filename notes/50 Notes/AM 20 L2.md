## Slide 1 · Why ODEs Cannot Always Be Solved Explicitly
#ODE #numerical-methods #algebraic-obstruction
%%
Recall ODEs are just equations which are equal to a rate of change in the variable of interest. 

I.e. Population of a cell culture is: immediate change in growth in the population over the immediate change in time =  a value which makes it a multiple of the prior value times the result of population growth over time. Such that we could find with the data of at time 0 pop was 40 that given at time 2 it became 80 that we could find that the rate of exponential growth was intrinsically related to how it grew over time linearly. So some of these ordinary (single sol.) equation involving [[Differentials|differentials]] don't naturally have explicit (See [[explicit solution]]) that is 
%%


Many [[ODE|ODEs]] — including practically important ones — **cannot be solved explicitly**. Two reasons:

1. **Algebraic reasons** — the steps needed to isolate or simplify the equation can't be completed in [[closed form]].
2. **Can't compute the necessary integrals** — even when the algebra is easy, simple functions often lack [[elementary antiderivatives]].

> "Some very simple functions don't have very simple antiderivatives … very often we use [[Numerical Methods|numerical methods]]."

#### Notes
1. The Trig "Algebraic Knot" (Can't Isolate $y'$)

**The Problem:** $y' + \sin(y') = x$

- **The Roadblock:** You cannot put this in **Normal Form**. If you try to solve for the rate of change $y'$, you are stuck in a loop: to get $y'$ out of the $\sin$ function, you have to use $\arcsin$, which then traps the $y'$ on the left inside the $\arcsin$.
- **The Result:** You can't even define the "Rule" clearly enough to start the calculus.
---

2. The Euler "Integration Wall" (Can't Find the Path)

**The Problem:** $y' = e^{x^2}$
- **The Roadblock:** This is already in perfect Normal Form, but it hits the "No-Name" wall. While the rule is simple—"my speed is $e^{x^2}$"—the integral $\int e^{x^2} dx$ is **non-elementary**.
- **The Result:** No combination of basic math symbols (powers, logs, trig) can ever write down the solution $y$. It is a perfectly valid behavior that simply has no "alphabetical" name in standard math.
---

## Slide 3 · Definition — Direction Field (Slope Field)
#direction-field #slope-field #ODE #definition

> **A [[Direction Field|direction field]]** (also called a **slope field**) for a first-order [[ODE]] $y' = f(t,y)$ is a rectangular grid of short arrows, where the arrow at grid point $(t_i, y_j)$ has slope $f(t_i, y_j)$.

Each arrow is *tangent* to whichever solution curve passes through that point. The picture gives a portrait of all solutions at once over a region.

### Notes









---

## Slide 4 · Arrow Tangency and the IVP Interpretation
#direction-field #IVP #integral-curve #existence-and-uniqueness

The arrow at $(t_i, y_j)$ is tangent to the **unique solution** of the [[Initial Value Problem|IVP]]:
$$y' = f(t,y), \quad y(t_i) = y_j$$
pointing in the direction of increasing $t$.

> "It's the slope of the graph — which is why this is also called a [[Direction Field|slope field]]."

Each grid point is simultaneously a starting condition (initial value) and a local tangent direction. Uniqueness is guaranteed by the [[Existence and Uniqueness|Existence & Uniqueness Theorem]].

### Notes

## Slide 5 · Creating a Direction Field in MATLAB — Basic Syntax
#MATLAB #direction-field #meshgrid #quiver

Reference: *Second MATLAB tutorial, Section 5*

```matlab
[T Y] = meshgrid(minT:step:maxT, minY:step:maxY);
dY = f(T, Y);          % vertical component — from the ODE
dT = ones(size(dY));   % horizontal component — always 1
quiver(T, Y, dT, dY);
```

| Variable | Role |
|---|---|
| `[T Y]` | Grid of base points — tails of arrows |
| `dY` | Vertical component from $f(t,y)$ |
| `dT` | Horizontal component (vector of ones) |
| `quiver` | Draws arrows |

> **Note:** Use element-wise `.*` (dot-multiply), not matrix multiply, since `Y` is a matrix.

## Slide 7 · Adjustment (i) — Rescale Arrows to Unit Length
#MATLAB #direction-field #quiver #rescaling

**Problem:** arrows of varying length make direction hard to read.

**Fix:** normalise to unit arrows.
```matlab
L = sqrt(1 + dY.^2);
quiver(T, Y, dT./L, dY./L);
```

Dividing each component by the arrow's length $L$ makes every arrow the same length, emphasising *direction* over *magnitude*.

> "Now the arrows all look a little bit longer — in particular they're all unit arrows."

### Notes









---

## Slide 8 · Adjustment (ii) — Remove White Space and Rescale Again
#MATLAB #direction-field #axis-tight

```matlab
quiver(T, Y, dT./L, dY./L, 0.5);  % 0.5 = scale factor, shortens arrows
axis tight                          % removes padding
```

| Command | Effect |
|---|---|
| `0.5` (4th arg) | Shrinks arrows to half length |
| `axis tight` | Sets limits exactly to data range |

## Slide 10 · Example 2 — Direction Field for Trigonometric ODE
#direction-field #MATLAB #trig-ODE #integral-curve #example

$$y' = \sin(\pi(t + y))$$

```matlab
[T Y] = meshgrid(0:0.125:3, 0:0.125:3);
dY = sin(pi*(T+Y));
dT = ones(size(dY));
L = sqrt(1 + dY.^2);
quiver(T, Y, dT./L, dY./L, 0.5);
axis tight
```

**Key observation:** solutions appear to approach a straight line with slope $-1$.

**Why?** On the line $t + y = n + \tfrac{1}{2}$ (odd half-integer), $\sin(\pi(t+y)) = -1$. So $y' = -1$ matches the slope of the line — it *is* a solution. Example: the line through $(2.5, 1)$ with slope $-1$.

This line is an [[Integral Curve|integral curve]] and an [[Asymptotic Stability|attractor]] for nearby solutions.

## Slide 11 · Definition — Integral Curve
#integral-curve #IVP #direction-field #definition

> **An [[Integral Curve|integral curve]]** is the graph of a *specific* solution of a differential equation — i.e., the solution of an [[Initial Value Problem]] with a particular initial condition.

Geometrically: a curve that is **tangent at every point** $(t, y(t))$ to the [[Direction Field|direction field]] arrow there.

[[Integral Curve|Integral curves]] are drawn using `ode45` in MATLAB (not by solving explicitly), since many [[ODE|ODEs]] have no closed-form solution.

## Slide 13 · Definition — Equilibrium Solution
#equilibrium-solution #ODE #definition #constant-solution

> **An [[Equilibrium Solution|equilibrium solution]]** of $y' = f(t,y)$ is a constant function $y = c$ that satisfies the equation:
> $$f(t, c) = 0 \quad \text{for all } t$$

Because $y = c$ is constant, $y' = 0$ identically, so the condition is just the right-hand side equaling zero.

> "When all those forces are in balance there is no motion, nothing is changing."

**General rule:** $y = c$ is an [[Equilibrium Solution|equilibrium]] of $y' = f(t,y)$ if $f(t,c) = 0$ for all $t$.

## Slide 15 · Existence and Uniqueness — Solutions Don't Intersect
#existence-and-uniqueness #IVP #direction-field #trapping

> **Key Theorem (preview).** Under reasonable assumptions on $f(t,y)$, the [[IVP]] $y' = f(t,y)$, $y(t_0) = y_0$ has a **unique solution**.

See: [[Existence and Uniqueness]]

**Consequence 1:** different solution curves **never intersect**. If two curves met at $(t^*, y^*)$, that point would have two different solutions — contradicting uniqueness.

**Consequence 2:** [[Equilibrium Solution|equilibrium]] lines **trap** solutions. A solution starting between two equilibria stays between them forever.

> "Under reasonable assumptions … the different solutions of the differential equation don't intersect."

## Slide 17 · Example 3 — Non-Intersection and Trapping Regions

Since solutions cannot cross ([[Existence and Uniqueness]]), any solution stays in its starting interval:

| Starting region | Solution stays in |
|---|---|
| $y < 0$ | $(-\infty, 0)$ |
| $0 < y < 2$ | $(0, 2)$ |
| $2 < y < 5$ | $(2, 5)$ |
| $y > 5$ | $(5, \infty)$ |

> "The [[Equilibrium Solution|equilibrium]] lines trap solutions."

## Slide 18 · Observation — Sign of $y'$ Between Equilibria
#sign-analysis #equilibrium-solution #direction-field #stability

$f(y) = 0.1y(y-2)(y-5)$ is continuous and zero *only* at $y = 0, 2, 5$, so it cannot change sign within any interval between them.

| Interval | Test value | Sign of $y'$ | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| $(-\infty, 0)$ | $y = -1$ | negative | ↓ |
| $(0, 2)$ | $y = 1$ | positive | ↑ |
| $(2, 5)$ | $y = 3$ | negative | ↓ |
| $(5, \infty)$ | $y = 6$ | positive | ↑ |

> "In between [[Equilibrium Solution|equilibria]] it's either positive or it's negative — it can't wiggle around zero."

This sign table directly determines the [[Asymptotic Stability|stability]] of each [[Equilibrium Solution|equilibrium]].

## Slide 22 · Definition — Asymptotic Stability
#asymptotic-stability #equilibrium-solution #long-run-behaviour #definition

> **An [[Equilibrium Solution|equilibrium]] $y = y_0$ is [[Asymptotic Stability|asymptotically stable]]** if solutions starting *sufficiently close* to $y_0$ converge to $y_0$ as $t \to \infty$:
> $$y(t_0) \approx y_0 \implies \lim_{t\to\infty} y(t) = y_0$$

The [[Equilibrium Solution|equilibrium]] acts like a **drain** — nearby solutions are pulled toward it.

**Note:** "close" is relative. If the nearest [[Equilibrium Solution|equilibrium]] is only 0.1 away, the basin of attraction is narrow.

> "If I start close enough to 2, then I'm going to return to 2."

Contrast with instability → [[#Slide 23 · Definition — Instability|Slide 23]].

### Notes









---

## Slide 23 · Definition — Instability
#instability #equilibrium-solution #long-run-behaviour #definition

> **An [[Equilibrium Solution|equilibrium]] $y = y_0$ is unstable** if solutions starting anywhere *other than* exactly $y_0$ move away and stay away:
> $$y(t_0) \ne y_0 \implies |y(t) - y_0| \not\to 0$$

**Important:** instability does *not* require solutions to blow up to $\pm\infty$. They just don't return.

> "It doesn't have to shoot off to infinity."

Contrast with [[Asymptotic Stability]] → [[#Slide 22 · Definition — Asymptotic Stability|Slide 22]].

## Slide 24 · Example — Stability Diagram for Example 3
#asymptotic-stability #instability #equilibrium-solution #sign-analysis #example

$$y' = 0.1y^3 - 0.7y^2 + y, \quad \text{equilibria: } y = 0, 2, 5$$

| Equilibrium | Nearby behaviour | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| $y = 0$ | Away both sides ($\downarrow$ below, $\uparrow$ above) | **Unstable** |
| $y = 2$ | Attracted both sides ($\uparrow$ below, $\downarrow$ above) | **[[Asymptotic Stability\|Asymptotically Stable]]** |
| $y = 5$ | Away both sides ($\downarrow$ below, $\uparrow$ above) | **Unstable** |

**Quick sign rule:**
- $y'$ changes $- \to +$ across [[Equilibrium Solution|equilibrium]] → **[[Asymptotic Stability|stable]]** (valley)
- $y'$ changes $+ \to -$ or same sign → **unstable** (hill or slope)

> "You can reverse the stability of the [[Equilibrium Solution|equilibria]] by multiplying the right-hand side by $-1$ — same equilibria, reversed stability."

## Slide 25 · Back to Solving — Separable Equation $y' = ay$
#separable-ODE #general-solution #equilibrium-solution #separation-of-variables

$$y' = ay \quad (a \text{ constant})$$

**Separation of variables:**
$$\frac{1}{y}\,dy = a\,dt \xrightarrow{\int} \ln|y| = at + C \xrightarrow{\exp} y = be^{at}, \quad b \in \mathbb{R}$$

$b = 0$ recovers the [[Equilibrium Solution|equilibrium]] $y = 0$ — it is included in the general formula.

> "The correct answer is $b$ can be anything — any real number."

### Notes

## Slide 26 · Generalising — Variable Coefficient $y' = a(t)y$
#separable-ODE #variable-coefficient #general-solution #integrating-factor

$$y' = a(t)\,y$$

Same steps, $a$ now a function:
$$\frac{1}{y}\,dy = a(t)\,dt \xrightarrow{\int} \ln|y| = A(t) + C \implies y = be^{A(t)}, \quad b \in \mathbb{R}$$

where $A'(t) = a(t)$ (any antiderivative of $a$). $b = 0$ gives $y = 0$ (the [[Equilibrium Solution|equilibrium]]).

Alternative derivation via [[Integrating-Factor|Integrating Factor]] → [[#Slide 27 · Method 2 — Integrating Factor for $y' - a(t)y = 0$|Slide 27]].

### Notes

## Slide 27 · Method 2 — Integrating Factor for $y' - a(t)y = 0$
#integrating-factor #linear-ODE #product-rule #method

Rewrite as linear [[ODE]]: $y' - a(t)y = 0$.

**Key idea:** find $\mu(t)$ such that $\mu y' - \mu a y = (\mu y)'$ — the product rule in reverse.

$$\mu(t) = e^{-A(t)}, \quad A'(t) = a(t)$$

Multiply through:
$$e^{-A(t)}y' - a(t)e^{-A(t)}y = 0 \implies \bigl[e^{-A(t)} \cdot y\bigr]' = 0$$
$$\implies e^{-A(t)} y = C \implies y = Ce^{A(t)}$$

Same result as separation of variables → [[#Slide 26 · Generalising — Variable Coefficient $y' = a(t)y$|Slide 26]].

> "Why do this instead of something else? Because this works … this method works for any first-order linear [[ODE]]."

### Notes









---

## Slide 28 · Terminology — Integrating Factor
#integrating-factor #linear-ODE #terminology #definition

> **The [[Integrating-Factor|integrating factor]]** for $y' - a(t)y = 0$ is $\mu(t) = e^{-A(t)}$, where $A'(t) = a(t)$.

**Why not include $+C$ in $A(t)$?** Adding a constant multiplies $\mu$ by a non-zero constant that cancels out in the final solution — choose the simplest antiderivative.

> "You might as well choose the simplest antiderivative — you don't have to choose all of them."

Generalises on Friday to the full first-order linear [[ODE]]: $y' + p(t)y = g(t)$.

## Slide 29 · Example — Solve IVP $y' = 2y\cos x$, $y(0) = 3$
#integrating-factor #IVP #worked-example #linear-ODE

**Step 1:** Rewrite: $y' - 2\cos(x)\,y = 0$

**Step 2:** [[Integrating-Factor|Integrating factor]]: $\displaystyle\int -2\cos x\,dx = -2\sin x \implies \mu(x) = e^{-2\sin x}$

**Step 3:** Multiply and apply product rule:
$$\bigl[e^{-2\sin x}\cdot y\bigr]' = 0 \implies e^{-2\sin x}\cdot y = C$$

**Step 4:** General solution: $y = Ce^{2\sin x}$

**Step 5:** Apply initial condition — $y(0) = 3 = C \cdot e^0 = C$

$$\boxed{y = 3e^{2\sin x}}$$

Alternatively, separation of variables gives the same answer. Both methods work.
