## Slide: Opportunity Cost

**[[Opportunity Cost]]** of producing something = the cost of *not* producing something else with those same resources.

*Example:* Workers can produce roses or computers.
- OC of producing computers = roses not produced
- OC of producing roses = computers not produced

> 📌 See [[Opportunity Cost]] for full derivation including the $a_{LC}/a_{LW}$ formula.

## Slide: Comparative Advantage — US vs. Colombia Example

*Setup:* In the U.S., producing 10M roses uses the same resources as 100,000 computers. In Colombia, producing 10M roses uses the same resources as 30,000 computers.

| | OC of 10M roses |
|---|---|
| U.S. | 100,000 computers |
| Colombia | 30,000 computers |

- U.S. has **lower OC of computers** → [[Comparative Advantage]] in computers
- Colombia has **lower OC of roses** → CA in roses

**Specialization gains (table from slide):**

| | Million Roses | Thousand Computers |
|---|---|---|
| United States | −10 | +100 |
| Colombia | +10 | −30 |
| **Total** | **0** | **+70** |

When each country specializes in its CA, 70,000 additional computers are produced at no cost in roses.

## Slide: Babe Ruth — Absolute vs. Comparative Advantage

Ruth had an [[Absolute Advantage]] in *pitching* (best left-handed pitcher in the American League, 89 wins, 29⅔ scoreless innings in World Series). But his CA was **batting**:
- As pitcher, he couldn't bat every game (rest required)
- The Red Sox moved him to center field in 1919 to exploit his CA → 29 HRs (then-record)
- Yankees kept him in the outfield → 54 HRs in 1920, untouched slugging record

**Lesson:** Exploit comparative advantage, not just absolute advantage.

## Slide: Can Comparative Advantage Be Created?

*Canada's Icewine* — cold climate is a comparative disadvantage for most wine but enables icewine production unavailable elsewhere. Natural conditions create CA.

*Walker Hanlon (UCLA) study:* U.S. Civil War cut British cotton supply → British firms forced to switch to Indian cotton → surge in British patents for new processes → Indian cotton prices recovered to pre-war levels via induced technological progress.

> CA can be created through necessity-driven innovation.

## Slide: Practice Question — Wheat and Beef (U.S. vs. Argentina)

| | Wheat (output/worker) | Beef (output/worker) |
|---|---|---|
| U.S. | 100 | 200 |
| Argentina | 200 | 400 |

**Answers:**
1. *Absolute advantage:* Argentina produces more of both → AA in both goods
2. *OC of wheat:* U.S. = 200/100 = **2 units beef**; Argentina = 400/200 = **2 units beef** — identical
3. *OC of beef:* U.S. = 100/200 = **0.5 units wheat**; Argentina = 200/400 = **0.5 units wheat** — identical
4. *Comparative advantage:* **Neither** — equal OC in both countries → no CA → no gains from trade

> 📌 Key insight: OC of one good = **reciprocal** of OC of the other good.

## Slide: Model Components

Any model has three elements:
1. **Exogenous parameters** — taken as given (e.g., $a, b, c, d$ in supply/demand)
2. **Endogenous outcomes** — determined within the model (e.g., $P, Q$)
3. **Equilibrium concept** — rules defining what endogenous outcomes satisfy given exogenous parameters (e.g., Supply = Demand)

## Slide: Ricardian Model Assumptions

World: two goods (Wine $W$, Clothing $C$); two countries (Home, Foreign).

| Assumption | Details |

| Single factor | Labor only |
| Fixed labor supply | $L$ constant in each country |
| Constant returns to scale | Linear [[Production Possibility Frontier\|PPF]] |

| Perfectly competitive markets | Producers take prices & wages as given; workers mobile across sectors |

## Slide: Production Technology — PPF Derivation

*Example:* $X = \frac{1}{2}L$ (clothing), $Y = 2L$ (wine), $L = 16$ workers.

| $L_C$ | $Q_C$ | $L_W$ | $Q_W$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 16 | 32 |
| 1 | 0.5 | 15 | 30 |
| 15 | 7.5 | 1 | 2 |
| 16 | 8 | 0 | 0 |

→ Plotting these gives a straight-line PPF. All points lie on the same line.

> 📌 See [[Production Possibility Frontier]] for the derivation and equation.

## Slide: PPF — Formal Equation

$$a_{LC}Q_C + a_{LW}Q_W = L$$

Rearranged:
$$Q_W = -\frac{a_{LC}}{a_{LW}}Q_C + \frac{L}{a_{LW}}$$

- **Slope** = $-\frac{a_{LC}}{a_{LW}}$ = [[Marginal Rate of Transformation|MRT]] = [[Opportunity Cost]] of clothing (depends only on *technology*, not endowment)
- **Y-intercept** = $\frac{L}{a_{LW}}$ (max wine)
- **X-intercept** = $\frac{L}{a_{LC}}$ (max clothing)

Increasing production of X by 1 unit pulls $a_{LC}$ workers from wine → wine falls by $\frac{a_{LC}}{a_{LW}}$.

<!-- space for your notes -->

## Slide: PPF is Linear — MPL Constant

$$MPL_X = \frac{\partial X}{\partial L} = \frac{1}{2}$$

Adding more workers to clothing increases output at a *constant* rate — constant returns to scale → linear PPF. (Contrast: diminishing returns → concave PPF.)

<!-- space for your notes -->

## Slide: Comparison — Non-Linear (Concave) PPF

**[[Marginal Rate of Transformation|MRT]]** = slope of PPF = how much extra of one good per unit sacrifice of the other.

With multiple factors and diminishing marginal returns, adding factors to good X yields *decreasing* additional output → PPF bows outward (concave). This is the shape in later models (Specific Factors, H-O).

## Slide: PPF Drawing Example

*Given:* 100 workers, $a_{LC} = 0.5$, $a_{LW} = 0.2$

- Max wine: $\frac{100}{0.2} = 500$ (y-intercept)
- Max cloth: $\frac{100}{0.5} = 200$ (x-intercept)
- Connect → linear PPF
- Slope = $-\frac{0.5}{0.2} = -2.5$

## Slide: Changing Technology

If clothing production technology improves ($a_{LC}$ falls):
- X-intercept ($L/a_{LC}$) shifts **right** (more max clothing)
- Y-intercept ($L/a_{LW}$) unchanged
- Slope **flattens** (OC of clothing falls)
- → PPF rotates outward on the clothing axis

<!-- space for your notes -->

## Slide: Changing Endowments

If labor $L$ increases:
- Both intercepts scale up proportionally
- Slope $(-a_{LC}/a_{LW})$ **unchanged** (technology unchanged)
- PPF shifts **outward in parallel**

## Slide: Pop Quiz — US vs. Mexico

*Given:* U.S. (300 workers), Mexico (400 workers)

| | U.S. | Mexico |
|---|---|---|
| $a_{LC}$ (Computers) | 1 | 8 |
| $a_{LA}$ (Avocados) | 3 | 4 |

**Answers:**
1. *Absolute advantage:* U.S. has AA in both ($a_{LC} < a_{LC}^*$ and $a_{LA} < a_{LA}^*$)
2. *Comparative advantage:*
   - OC of computers: U.S. = $1/3$; Mexico = $8/4 = 2$ → U.S. has CA in computers
   - OC of avocados: U.S. = $3$; Mexico = $1/2$ → Mexico has CA in avocados
3. *PPF:*
   - U.S.: intercepts at $Q_A = 300/3 = 100$, $Q_C = 300/1 = 300$
   - Mexico: intercepts at $Q_A = 400/4 = 100$, $Q_C = 400/8 = 50$

## Slide: Production and Relative Supply

Given the PPF, optimal production maximizes the **value of production** subject to the PPF:
$$\max_{Q_C, Q_W} \; P_C Q_C + P_W Q_W \quad \text{s.t. } a_{LC}Q_C + a_{LW}Q_W = L$$

The value-of-production iso-revenue line:
$$Q_W = -\frac{P_C}{P_W} Q_C + \frac{Y}{P_W}$$

Slope = $-P_C/P_W$ (relative price of clothing). Optimal production is where the highest iso-revenue line touches the PPF.

| Condition | Optimal Production |
|---|---|
| $P_C/P_W < a_{LC}/a_{LW}$ | Specialize in **Wine** (slope of value < slope of PPF) |
| $P_C/P_W = a_{LC}/a_{LW}$ | Any point on PPF |
| $P_C/P_W > a_{LC}/a_{LW}$ | Specialize in **Clothing** |

## Slide: Relative Supply Curve Derivation

| Condition | $Q_C, Q_W$ | $Q_C/Q_W$ |
|---|---|---|
| $P_C/P_W < a_{LC}/a_{LW}$ | $Q_W = L/a_{LW},\; Q_C = 0$ | 0 |
| $P_C/P_W = a_{LC}/a_{LW}$ | Any combo on PPF | $[0, \infty]$ |
| $P_C/P_W > a_{LC}/a_{LW}$ | $Q_C = L/a_{LC},\; Q_W = 0$ | undefined |

The single-country RS is a **step function** (flat at zero, then vertical, then flat at infinity) at $P_C/P_W = a_{LC}/a_{LW}$.

## Slide: Consumption — Budget Constraint

Consumer chooses $(D_C, D_W)$ to maximize utility subject to:
$$P_C D_C + P_W D_W \leq I$$

Rearranged:
$$D_W = \frac{I}{P_W} - \frac{P_C}{P_W} D_C$$

Slope = $-P_C/P_W$. Y-intercept = $I/P_W$ (max wine if all income on wine).

<!-- space for your notes -->

## Slide: Preferences and Utility Functions

Preferences represented by utility function $U(D_C, D_W)$ or graphically by **indifference curves**.

Key assumptions:
- **Monotonicity:** $MU_C > 0$, $MU_W > 0$ (more is better)
- **Diminishing marginal utility:** $\partial MU_C / \partial D_C < 0$
- **[[Marginal Rate of Substitution|MRS]]** = slope of indifference curve = $MU_C / MU_W$
- **Decreasing MRS:** as wine consumption rises, willingness to give up wine for clothing rises → preference for variety

## Slide: Optimal Consumption

Optimal consumption $(D_C^*, D_W^*)$ satisfies:
1. $MRS = P_C/P_W$ (tangency: budget constraint slope = indifference curve slope)
2. $P_C D_C + P_W D_W = I$ (on budget constraint)

## Slide: Consumption and Relative Demand

When $P_C/P_W$ falls (clothing cheaper):
- Income and substitution effects change $D_C$ and $D_W$
- Individual quantities ambiguous, but $D_C/D_W$ **rises** in general
- → RD curve **slopes downward**

$$\frac{D_C}{D_W} = RD\left(\frac{P_C}{P_W}, I\right)$$

## Slide: Autarky Equilibrium

[[Autarky]] = country closed to trade. Must satisfy:
1. Firms maximize profits
2. Consumers maximize utility
3. Markets clear (supply = demand in each good)

The Ricardian model is special: autarky price is **pinned by supply** alone:
$$\left(\frac{P_C}{P_W}\right)^{autarky} = \frac{a_{LC}}{a_{LW}}$$

[[Relative Demand|Relative demand]] determines only the equilibrium quantity mix.

Real example: U.S. Jeffersonian Embargo (1807–1809) — near-autarky imposed a welfare cost of ~5% of GDP.
