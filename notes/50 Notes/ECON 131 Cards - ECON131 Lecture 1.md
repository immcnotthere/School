# ECON 131 Cards - ECON131 Lecture 1 Globalization and MNCs

## Multinational Corporation (MNC)
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is a multinational corporation (MNC)? | A firm with a parent company in its home (originating) country and operating subsidiaries, branches, or affiliates in other countries. | p.15; definition |
| How does an MNC's foreign subsidiary differ from simple exporting? | A subsidiary is a real operating presence abroad (production, sales, or service unit); exporting just sells home-produced goods into a foreign market with no local operating presence. | p.15; discrimination |

## MNC Foreign Market Entry Modes
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What does licensing mean as a foreign market entry mode? | The MNC gives a local firm the right to manufacture its product in exchange for fees; the MNC does not operate abroad itself. | p.15; definition |
| What does franchising mean as a foreign market entry mode? | The MNC provides its sales or service strategies/brand to a local operator abroad in exchange for fees. | p.15; definition |
| How does a joint venture differ from licensing or franchising? | Two or more firms form a new, jointly owned legal entity abroad, rather than one firm just licensing rights or a brand to the other. | p.15; discrimination |
| What is FDI as an entry mode, and how does greenfield investment differ from it? | FDI: the MNC sets up production facilities abroad by establishing new operations or acquiring an existing business. Greenfield specifically means starting the company entirely from scratch. | p.15; discrimination |

## Trade Openness
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What does trade openness measure? | The size of a country's trade (exports plus imports) relative to its GDP; how exposed an economy is to international trade. | p.10; definition |
| How has world trade openness trended since 1970? | It rose from about 25% of GDP in 1970 to roughly 55 to 60% by the 2000s to 2010s, though it dipped sharply around 2009. | p.10; definition |
| Why can small economies like Hong Kong or Singapore show trade openness above 100% of GDP while the US is much lower? | Small economies rely heavily on trade with the rest of the world and often re-export goods, so trade volume can exceed their own GDP; large economies like the US produce mostly for their own large domestic market. | p.11; discrimination |

## Financial Openness (Capital Account Openness)
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What does financial (capital account) openness measure? | How freely capital can flow into and out of a country across borders, i.e. how open a country's capital account is. | p.14; definition |
| How does financial openness differ across country groups since the 1970s? | Industrial countries have risen to near-full openness (close to 1); emerging markets and less-developed countries have opened more slowly and remain well below full openness. | p.14; discrimination |

## Sudden Stop (Capital Flows)
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is a sudden stop in international finance? | An abrupt, sharp reversal or halt of capital inflows into a country, which can trigger a currency crisis or credit crunch. | p.20; definition |
| Why is a sudden stop a cost of financial openness rather than trade openness? | It is about foreign capital fleeing a country's financial markets or funding, not about goods and services trade. | p.20; discrimination |

## Trade Globalization Benefits and Costs
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What are the main benefits of trade globalization mentioned in lecture? | Lower goods prices for consumers and access to larger foreign markets for firms. | p.17; definition |
| What is the main cost of trade globalization mentioned in lecture? | Revenue and job losses for some local manufacturers exposed to import competition. | p.17; definition |
| What did Acemoglu et al. (2016) estimate about rising Chinese import competition? | That it accounted for roughly 20% of the estimated job losses from that channel of import competition. | p.17; definition |

## Financial Globalization Benefits and Costs
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What are the two main benefits of financial globalization (openness) from lecture? | Domestic recessions can be buffered through borrowing from abroad, and the cost of capital decreases. | p.20; definition |
| What are the two main costs of financial globalization (openness) from lecture? | Sudden stops (abrupt capital flow reversals) and tax evasion. | p.20; definition |

## 2018 US-China Trade War (Tariff Escalation)
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What was the sequence of the 2018 US-China tariff escalation? | March 2, 2018: US raised tariffs on steel and aluminum. March 24, 2018: US imposed large tariffs on Chinese imports. April 2, 2018: China retaliated with tariffs on 128 US products. | p.18; procedure |
| What cause-effect pattern does the 2018 trade war illustrate? | Unilateral tariff increases by one country provoke retaliatory tariffs from the trading partner, escalating protectionism on both sides. | p.18; discrimination |

# Frankenstein problems

Numbers in these problems are illustrative (made up for practice), not figures from the lecture.

**Problem 1**
Country X is a small open economy with trade openness of about 110% of GDP. It has just fully liberalized its capital account after decades of restrictions. Shortly after, a global risk-off shock hits emerging markets broadly.

(a) Why is Country X's measured trade openness so high, above 100% of GDP?
(b) What new financial vulnerability does opening the capital account create for Country X, and what triggered it in this scenario?
(c) Is the vulnerability in (b) best classified as a cost of trade globalization or financial globalization?

**Solution**
(a) Small economies trade heavily relative to the size of their own economy and often re-export goods, so total trade volume can exceed domestic GDP; large economies produce more for their own big domestic market, so trade is a smaller share of GDP.
(b) Liberalizing the capital account exposes the country to a sudden stop: an abrupt reversal of capital inflows. Here it is triggered by a global risk-off shock (investors pulling back from emerging markets).
(c) It is a cost of financial globalization (financial openness), not trade globalization, because it concerns capital and funding flows, not goods and services trade.

Concepts tested: Trade Openness; Financial Openness (Capital Account Openness); Sudden Stop (Capital Flows).

**Problem 2**
A US shoe manufacturer currently exports shoes made in the US to China. On April 2, 2018, China imposes retaliatory tariffs on US goods (including shoes) after the US had raised tariffs on Chinese imports on March 24, 2018. The firm considers switching from exporting to forming a joint venture with a Chinese manufacturer instead.

(a) Why would switching from exporting to a joint venture reduce the firm's exposure to China's new tariffs?
(b) Define a joint venture as an entry mode.
(c) What domestic cost of trade globalization might this shift create back in the US?

**Solution**
(a) Under exporting, the shoes physically cross the border and are subject to China's tariffs on US imports (the April 2, 2018 retaliation). Under a joint venture, the shoes would be produced and sold within China by the jointly owned local entity, so they are not classified as imports facing the new tariff.
(b) A joint venture is when two or more firms form a new legal entity abroad that is jointly owned by all of the firms involved.
(c) Shifting production to a China-based joint venture would mean less shoe manufacturing happens in the US, so it could cause the same kind of revenue and job losses for US manufacturing workers that trade globalization is described as creating for exposed domestic manufacturers.

Concepts tested: MNC Foreign Market Entry Modes; 2018 US-China Trade War (Tariff Escalation); Trade Globalization Benefits and Costs.
