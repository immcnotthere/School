# Lecture 11 — 2nd Order Linear Homogeneous ODEs II

## Outline

- Equations with constant coefficients
- The characteristic equation
- Case 1: two distinct real roots
- Case 2: one repeated real root
- Case 3: complex conjugate roots

---

## Setup

We are solving the **constant coefficient** second-order linear homogeneous ODE:

$$\boxed{ay'' + by' + cy = 0}$$

**General theory** (established in prior lectures):

1. Solutions exist on all of $\mathbb{R}$
2. The set of solutions is a **2-dimensional vector space**

> [!note] What "2-dimensional solution space" means
> The general solution is $y = c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2$ for any constants $c_1, c_2$. Every solution is a linear combination of exactly two independent "basis" solutions. Our job is to find that **fundamental pair** $\{y_1, y_2\}$.

---

## The Characteristic Equation

### The key idea — guess $y = e^{rt}$

%%
The motivation for guessing $e^{rt}$: look back at the 1st order case.

In $\alpha y' + \beta y = 0$ (with $\alpha \neq 0$), the general solution is $y = ce^{rt}$ where $r = -\beta/\alpha$.
This came from solving $\alpha r + \beta = 0$.

So exponentials are natural. The question for 2nd order: what value of $r$ makes $e^{rt}$ a solution?
%%

**Observation:** In the 1st order case $\alpha y' + \beta y = 0$, the solution is $y = ce^{rt}$ where $r$ satisfies $\alpha r + \beta = 0$. Exponentials "survive" differentiation — a derivative of $e^{rt}$ is just a multiple of $e^{rt}$. So let's try the same idea for 2nd order.

**Ansatz:** look for solutions of $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$ of the form $y = e^{rt}$.

$$y = e^{rt} \implies y' = re^{rt} \implies y'' = r^2 e^{rt}$$

Substitute into the ODE:

$$a(r^2 e^{rt}) + b(re^{rt}) + c(e^{rt}) = 0$$

$$e^{rt}\underbrace{(ar^2 + br + c)}_{\text{must be zero}} = 0$$

Since $e^{rt} \neq 0$ for any $t$, we need:

$$\boxed{ar^2 + br + c = 0}$$

> [!definition] Characteristic Equation
> The **characteristic equation** of $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$ is
> $$ar^2 + br + c = 0$$
> Its roots $r$ are exactly the values that make $e^{rt}$ a solution.

### Three cases

The discriminant $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$ determines the nature of the roots:

| Discriminant | Case | Roots |
|---|---|---|
| $b^2 - 4ac > 0$ | Case 1 | Two distinct real roots $r_1 \neq r_2$ |
| $b^2 - 4ac = 0$ | Case 2 | One repeated real root $r_1 = r_2$ |
| $b^2 - 4ac < 0$ | Case 3 | Two complex conjugate roots $\lambda \pm i\mu$ |

---

## Case 1 — Two Distinct Real Roots

### Example 1

Solve $2y'' + y' - y = 0$.

**Step 1 — characteristic equation:**

$$2r^2 + r - 1 = 0$$

$$r = \frac{-1 \pm \sqrt{1 + 8}}{4} = \frac{-1 \pm 3}{4} \implies r_1 = \frac{1}{2}, \quad r_2 = -1$$

**Step 2 — two solutions:**

$$y_1 = e^{t/2}, \qquad y_2 = e^{-t}$$

**Step 3 — check they are independent (Wronskian):**

$$W(y_1, y_2) = \begin{vmatrix} e^{t/2} & e^{-t} \\ \frac{1}{2}e^{t/2} & -e^{-t} \end{vmatrix} = -e^{-t/2} - \frac{1}{2}e^{-t/2} = -\frac{3}{2}e^{-t/2} \neq 0 \text{ for all } t$$

%%
The Wronskian being nonzero for all $t$ confirms $\{y_1, y_2\}$ is a fundamental pair — the two solutions are linearly independent everywhere.
%%

**Step 4 — general (homogeneous) solution:**

$$y_h = c_1 e^{t/2} + c_2 e^{-t}$$

**Step 5 — IVP:** find the solution satisfying $y(0) = 1$, $y'(0) = -1$.

$$y = c_1 e^{t/2} + c_2 e^{-t}$$
$$y' = \tfrac{1}{2}c_1 e^{t/2} - c_2 e^{-t}$$

At $t = 0$:

$$y(0) = c_1 + c_2 = 1$$
$$y'(0) = \tfrac{1}{2}c_1 - c_2 = -1$$

Adding: $\tfrac{3}{2}c_1 = 0 \implies c_1 = 0$, then $c_2 = 1$.

$$\boxed{y = e^{-t}}$$

---

### General result — Case 1

> [!definition] Case 1 General Solution
> If $ar^2 + br + c = 0$ has **two distinct real roots** $r_1$ and $r_2$, then
> $$y_1 = e^{r_1 x}, \qquad y_2 = e^{r_2 x}$$
> form a fundamental pair of solutions, and the general solution is
> $$y = c_1 e^{r_1 x} + c_2 e^{r_2 x}$$

The Wronskian is $W(e^{r_1 x}, e^{r_2 x}) = (r_2 - r_1)e^{(r_1 + r_2)x} \neq 0$ since $r_1 \neq r_2$.

**IVP formula:** given $y(0) = y_0$, $y'(0) = y_0'$, solve:

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ r_1 & r_2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} y_0 \\ y_0' \end{pmatrix}$$

The determinant of the coefficient matrix is $r_2 - r_1 = W(e^{r_1 x}, e^{r_2 x})\big|_{x=0} \neq 0$, so the system always has a unique solution.

### Behavior of nonzero solutions

%%
This is important: knowing the sign of the roots tells you qualitatively what the solution does — does it blow up, decay, or do both depending on initial conditions?
%%

| Root signs | Behavior as $x \to +\infty$ |
|---|---|
| $r_1 > 0$ and $r_2 > 0$ | $y \to +\infty$ (both terms grow) |
| $r_1 > 0$ and $r_2 < 0$ | $y \to \pm\infty$ if $c_1 \neq 0$; $y \to 0$ if $c_1 = 0$ |
| $r_1 < 0$ and $r_2 < 0$ | $y \to 0$ (both terms decay) |

## Case 2 — One Repeated Real Root

### Example 2

Solve $y'' + 4y' + 4y = 0$.

**Characteristic equation:**

$$r^2 + 4r + 4 = (r + 2)^2 = 0 \implies r = -2 \quad \text{(repeated)}$$

**First solution:** $y_1 = e^{-2t}$.

But we need **two** independent solutions. We only have one from the characteristic equation — so where does the second come from?

### Variation of parameters

%%
"Variation of parameters" here means: take the solution we already have, $y_1 = e^{-2t}$, and multiply it by an unknown function $u(t)$ instead of a constant. If we can find $u$ such that $y_2 = u \cdot y_1$ satisfies the ODE, we get our second solution.

The insight: a constant times $y_1$ is just another multiple of $y_1$ — useless. But a *function* times $y_1$ can be independent.
%%

Let $y_2 = u(t) \cdot y_1 = u(t) \cdot e^{-2t}$ where $u$ is an unknown function.

Substitute $y_2'' + 4y_2' + 4y_2 = 0$. Using the product rule to expand $(u \cdot y_1)''$ and $(u \cdot y_1)'$:

$$(u \cdot y_1)' = u'y_1 + uy_1'$$
$$(u \cdot y_1)'' = u''y_1 + 2u'y_1' + uy_1''$$

Substituting into the ODE and collecting terms:

$$u\underbrace{(y_1'' + 4y_1' + 4y_1)}_{=\,0} + u''y_1 + 2u'y_1' + 4u'y_1 = 0$$

The big bracket vanishes because $y_1$ is already a solution. What remains, with $y_1 = e^{-2t}$ and $y_1' = -2e^{-2t}$:

$$u''e^{-2t} + 2u'(-2e^{-2t}) + 4u'e^{-2t} = 0$$

$$u''e^{-2t} + (-4 + 4)u'e^{-2t} = 0$$

$$u''e^{-2t} = 0 \implies u'' = 0$$

$$\implies u = ct + d$$

The simplest non-constant choice: $u = t$. Therefore:

$$\boxed{y_2 = te^{-2t}}$$

**Wronskian check:** $W(e^{-2t},\, te^{-2t}) = e^{-4t} \neq 0$ ✓

**General solution:**

$$y = c_1 e^{-2t} + c_2 te^{-2t}$$

**IVP:** $y(0) = 1$, $y'(0) = 0$.

$$y(0) = c_1 = 1$$
$$y' = -2e^{-2t} + c_2(e^{-2t} - 2te^{-2t})$$
$$y'(0) = -2 + c_2 = 0 \implies c_2 = 2$$

$$\boxed{y = e^{-2t} + 2te^{-2t}}$$

---

### General result — Case 2

> [!definition] Case 2 General Solution
> If $ar^2 + br + c = 0$ has **one repeated real root** $r$, then
> $$y_1 = e^{rx}, \qquad y_2 = xe^{rx}$$
> form a fundamental pair, and the general solution is
> $$y = c_1 e^{rx} + c_2 x e^{rx} = (c_1 + c_2 x)e^{rx}$$

> [!tip] Why $te^{rt}$ and not something else?
> The repeated root means the characteristic equation "wanted" to give two copies of $e^{rt}$, but they collapse into one. Multiplying by $t$ is the standard fix — it's the simplest function that produces an independent solution when the exponential alone is repeated. This same idea appears in solving repeated roots of algebraic systems.

### Behavior of nonzero solutions

| Root sign | Behavior as $x \to +\infty$ |
|---|---|
| $r < 0$ | $y \to 0$ (the $xe^{rx}$ term still decays — exponential wins over polynomial) |
| $r > 0$ | $y \to \pm\infty$ |

## Case 3 — Complex Conjugate Roots

### Euler's formula (recall)

> [!note] Euler's Formula
> $$e^{x + iw} = e^x\cos w + i\,e^x\sin w$$
> and its conjugate:
> $$e^{x - iw} = e^x\cos w - i\,e^x\sin w$$

%%
This is the bridge between the complex exponentials the characteristic equation gives us and the real-valued solutions we actually want.

$\cos w = \cos(-w)$ (cosine is even)
$\sin(-w) = -\sin w$ (sine is odd)
%%

### Example 3

Solve $y'' + 2y' + 5y = 0$.

**Characteristic equation:**

$$r^2 + 2r + 5 = 0 \implies r = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 20}}{2} = -1 \pm 2i$$

So $\lambda = -1$, $\mu = 2$.

**Complex-valued solutions:**

$$z_1 = e^{(-1+2i)x} = e^{-x}\cos 2x + i\,e^{-x}\sin 2x$$
$$z_2 = e^{(-1-2i)x} = e^{-x}\cos 2x - i\,e^{-x}\sin 2x$$

%%
These are valid solutions (complex-valued), but we want real-valued solutions since our ODE has real coefficients. The trick: linear combinations of solutions are solutions (superposition principle), so take real/imaginary parts.
%%

**Want real-valued solutions.** Linear combinations of solutions are solutions, so:

$$y_1 = \frac{z_1 + z_2}{2} = e^{-x}\cos 2x \qquad \text{(real part of } z_1\text{)}$$

$$y_2 = \frac{z_1 - z_2}{2i} = e^{-x}\sin 2x \qquad \text{(imaginary part of } z_1\text{)}$$

**Wronskian:** $W(e^{-x}\cos 2x,\, e^{-x}\sin 2x) = 2e^{-2x} \neq 0$ ✓

**General solution:**

$$y = e^{-x}(c_1\cos 2x + c_2\sin 2x)$$

**IVP:** $y(0) = 2$, $y'(0) = -1$.

$$y(0) = c_1 = 2$$

$$y' = -e^{-x}(c_1\cos 2x + c_2\sin 2x) + e^{-x}(-2c_1\sin 2x + 2c_2\cos 2x)$$

$$y'(0) = -c_1 + 2c_2 = -1 \implies -2 + 2c_2 = -1 \implies c_2 = \frac{1}{2}$$

$$\boxed{y = e^{-x}\!\left(2\cos 2x + \tfrac{1}{2}\sin 2x\right)}$$

---

### General result — Case 3

> [!definition] Case 3 General Solution
> If $ar^2 + br + c = 0$ has **complex conjugate roots** $\lambda \pm i\mu$ (with $\mu \neq 0$), then
> $$y_1 = e^{\lambda x}\cos\mu x, \qquad y_2 = e^{\lambda x}\sin\mu x$$
> form a fundamental pair, and the general solution is
> $$\boxed{y = e^{\lambda x}(c_1\cos\mu x + c_2\sin\mu x)}$$

### Behavior of nonzero solutions

The envelope $e^{\lambda x}$ controls the amplitude; the trig terms create oscillation.

| Sign of $\lambda$ | Behavior | Name |
|---|---|---|
| $\lambda < 0$ | $y \to 0$ — ==oscillations die out== | Damped oscillation |
| $\lambda = 0$ | $y$ bounded, oscillates forever — $\lim_{x\to\infty} y$ does not exist | Pure oscillation |
| $\lambda > 0$ | $y$ grows without bound — $\lim_{x\to\infty} y$ does not exist | Growing oscillation |

## Example 4 — Pure Oscillation

Solve $y'' + 9y = 0$, $y(0) = 2$, $y'(0) = 3$.

**Characteristic equation:**

$$r^2 + 9 = 0 \implies r = \pm 3i \qquad (\lambda = 0,\; \mu = 3)$$

**General solution:**

$$y = c_1\cos 3x + c_2\sin 3x$$

$$y' = -3c_1\sin 3x + 3c_2\cos 3x$$

**Initial conditions:**

$$y(0) = c_1 = 2$$
$$y'(0) = 3c_2 = 3 \implies c_2 = 1$$

$$y = 2\cos 3x + \sin 3x$$

%%
The professor notes this "looks like a shifted cosine" — and it is. Any sum $A\cos\omega x + B\sin\omega x$ can be written as $R\cos(\omega x - \beta)$ for suitable amplitude $R$ and phase $\beta$. This is the phase-amplitude form.
%%

### Phase-amplitude (polar) form

The solution $y = 2\cos 3x + \sin 3x$ can be rewritten as a single cosine with a phase shift.

**Recall:** $\cos(\theta - \beta) = \cos\theta\cos\beta + \sin\theta\sin\beta$, so:

$$R\cos(3x - \beta) = R\cos\beta\cdot\cos 3x + R\sin\beta\cdot\sin 3x$$

Matching coefficients with $2\cos 3x + 1\cdot\sin 3x$:

$$R\cos\beta = 2, \qquad R\sin\beta = 1$$

$$R^2 = R^2\cos^2\beta + R^2\sin^2\beta = 2^2 + 1^2 = 5 \implies R = \sqrt{5}$$

$$\cos\beta = \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}, \quad \sin\beta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \implies \beta \in \text{first quadrant}$$

$$\beta = \arccos\!\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}\right) \approx 0.464 \text{ radians}$$

$$\boxed{y = \sqrt{5}\,\cos(3x - 0.464)}$$

> [!tip] General phase-amplitude formula
> For any solution $y = A\cos\omega x + B\sin\omega x$:
> $$R = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2}, \qquad \beta = \arctan\!\left(\frac{B}{A}\right) \quad \text{(check quadrant!)}$$
> $$y = R\cos(\omega x - \beta)$$
> The amplitude is $R$ and the period is $2\pi/\omega$.

---

## Summary — The Three Cases

| Case | Discriminant | Roots | Fundamental pair | General solution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $b^2 - 4ac > 0$ | $r_1 \neq r_2$ real | $e^{r_1 x},\; e^{r_2 x}$ | $c_1 e^{r_1 x} + c_2 e^{r_2 x}$ |
| 2 | $b^2 - 4ac = 0$ | $r$ repeated | $e^{rx},\; xe^{rx}$ | $(c_1 + c_2 x)e^{rx}$ |
| 3 | $b^2 - 4ac < 0$ | $\lambda \pm i\mu$ | $e^{\lambda x}\cos\mu x,\; e^{\lambda x}\sin\mu x$ | $e^{\lambda x}(c_1\cos\mu x + c_2\sin\mu x)$ |

> [!warning] Don't forget the Wronskian check
> In each case, the Wronskian of the two proposed solutions is nonzero for all $x$. This is what guarantees they are a genuine fundamental pair and not two multiples of the same function. The Wronskian being nonzero means the system of equations for $c_1, c_2$ in an IVP always has a unique solution.
