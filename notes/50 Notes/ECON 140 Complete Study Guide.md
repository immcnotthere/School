### Key Conceptual Contrasts (Highest-yield for T/F and FRQ)

- **Specific Factors vs S-S:** Short run (industry-specific predictions) vs long run (factor-specific predictions). 1973 data supports SF not HO.

- **IRS T/F trap:** Larger markets do NOT gain more — smaller markets gain more. IRS model does NOT predict CA-based exports.

- **Ricardian T/F trap:** Productivity improvement in CA good does NOT improve terms of trade — it may worsen them (more supply lowers price).

- **Large vs small tariff:** Small = pure DWL, optimal = 0. Large = ToT gain possible, optimal > 0. But large economy can still lose if DWL > ToT gain.

- **Tariff vs quota:** Equivalent only if (a) same import quantity AND (b) rents stay domestic. Falls apart under uncertainty or when foreign firm holds quota license.

- **Horizontal vs vertical FDI:** Horizontal FDI preferred when trade costs HIGH, setup costs LOW (Brainard 1997). Vertical FDI = fragment steps to exploit wage differences.

### 1. Ricardian Model
**Total questions using this model: 11** (PS1 Q1 a–h, PS1 Q2, Practice Midterm Q1 a–f, Midterm T/F ×2)

**Lectures:** [[Lec1]] (intro, PPF review) · [[Lec2]] · [[Lec3]] · [[Lec4]] (Part III: relative wages, comparative statics) · [[Lec5]] (background)

**Purpose:** Trade driven by comparative advantage (CA), not absolute advantage. One factor (labor), CRS, perfect competition.

> [!note] Core Formulas
> - **PPF:** aLC·QC + aLF·QF = L → linear, slope = −aLC/aLF = opportunity cost of C in F
> - **Autarky price:** PC/PF = aLC/aLF (supply pins price)
> - **CA rule:** Home has CA in clothing iff aLC/aLF < aLC\*/aLF\*
> - **Relative wage:** w/w\* bounded by [aLF\*/aLF, aLC\*/aLC]

**Relative Supply (RS):** Step function — flat at Home's OC, vertical at complete specialization (QC = L/aLC), flat again at Foreign's OC.

**Relative Demand (RD):** Downward sloping. With DC/DF = k/(PC/PF), autarky equilibrium: PC/PF = aLC/aLF.

**World equilibrium:** World price between the two autarky prices → both specialize and gain. If RD intersects vertical segment → complete specialization on both sides.

> [!tip] Comparative Statics
> - **L increase:** RS shifts right → world price falls toward that country's autarky price → their gains from trade shrink, partner's grow. If price hits autarky price → incomplete specialization.
> - **Productivity improvement in CA good:** RS vertical segment shifts right (more supply), flat segment shifts down (lower autarky price) → terms of trade may fall or stay flat, not rise. Welfare may still rise (PPF expands).

> [!warning] Common Traps
> - L endowment does NOT affect CA direction — only aLC/aLF ratios matter
> - Productivity gain in CA good may WORSEN terms of trade (immiserizing growth)
> - RS is a step function with two kinks — build it with numeric values

**Graph:** PC/PF on y-axis, QC/QF on x-axis. RS is step function with two kinks at each country's autarky price. RD is downward-sloping hyperbola. Intersection = world equilibrium.

##### [Practice Midterm] Q1: Home/Foreign, Clothing/Food

**Setup:** aLC = 3, aLF = 2, L = 150; aLC\* = 5, aLF\* = 10, L\* = 200. RD = 5/(PC/PF).

**Autarky prices:** Home: PC/PF = 3/2 = **1.5.** Foreign: PC\*/PF\* = 5/10 = **0.5.**
Foreign has CA in clothing (OC = 0.5 < Home's 1.5). **Home has CA in food.**

**PPF:** Home: 3QC + 2QF = 150 → max QC = 50, max QF = 75. Foreign: 5QC\* + 10QF\* = 200 → max QC\* = 40, max QF\* = 20.

**World RS:** Vertical segment at QC/QF = 40/75. Flat at 0.5 (Foreign autarky), flat at 1.5 (Home autarky).

**Equilibrium:** Set 5/(PC/PF) = 40/75 → PC/PF = 9.375 → outside [0.5, 1.5]. RD intersects upper flat segment.
**PC/PF = 1.5, QC/QF = 10/3. Home incompletely specialized. Foreign fully specializes in clothing.**

**Relative wages:** w = PF/aLF = PF/2. w\* = PC/aLC\* = PC/5.
w/w\* = (PF/2)/(PC/5) = (PF/PC) × (5/2) = (1/1.5) × (5/2) = **5/3.**

**Population shock (L\* = 100):** RS vertical segment shifts left to QC/QF = 20/75. RD still intersects upper flat at PC/PF = 1.5. **Equilibrium unchanged.** Home absorbs change (produces more of both).

> [!tip] Exam Emphasis
> Step through: (1) autarky prices, (2) identify CA, (3) build RS with numeric kinks, (4) check where RD intersects — flat vs vertical, (5) flat segment → that country incompletely specialized, (6) relative wages from zero-profit conditions.

##### [PS1] Q1: US / Canada, Clothing / Food

**Setup:** US: L = 200, each hour produces 2C or 2F (aLC = 0.5, aLF = 0.5). Canada: L = 300, each hour produces 1C or 2F (aLC\* = 1, aLF\* = 0.5).

**(a) Absolute advantage:**
- US: 0.5 worker-hr per unit C vs Canada's 1 → US has absolute advantage in clothing.
- Both 0.5 worker-hr per unit F → neither has absolute advantage in food.

**(b) Opportunity costs and CA:**
- US: OC of C = 1F. OC of F = 1C. (aLC/aLF = 1)
- Canada: OC of C = 2F. OC of F = 0.5C. (aLC\*/aLF\* = 2)
- **US has CA in clothing (OC 1 < 2). Canada has CA in food (OC 0.5 < 1).**

**(c) PPF:**
- US: QC + QF = 400. Max QC = 400, max QF = 400. Slope = −1.
- Canada: 2QC\* + QF\* = 600. Max QC\* = 300, max QF\* = 600. Slope = −2.

**(e) Autarky equilibrium:**
- US RS flat at PC/PF = 1. With RD = 1/(PC/PF): DC/DF = 1 at PC/PF = 1. QC = QF = 200.
- Canada RS flat at PC/PF = 2. DC/DF = 0.5 at PC/PF = 2.

**(f) Free trade equilibrium:**
- World RS: flat at 1, vertical at QC/QF = 400/600 = 2/3, flat at 2.
- RD = 1/(PC/PF). At QC/QF = 2/3: DC/DF = 2/3 → PC/PF = 1.5. Since 1 < 1.5 < 2, intersects vertical.
- **World price PC/PF = 1.5. US fully specializes in C (400), Canada in F (600).**

**(g) Welfare:** Both gain — consume beyond their PPF at new price ratio.

**(h) US labor force × 1.5 (L = 300):**
- (i) New PPF: QC + QF = 600. Parallel outward shift.
- (ii) CA unchanged — depends only on aLC/aLF ratios, not L.
- (iii) RS shifts right. Price falls to PC/PF = 1 (US autarky price). **US no longer gains. Canada gains more.**

> [!tip] Exam Emphasis
> CA determined by ratios only. L endowment shifts RS horizontally but not autarky prices. Growth shock erodes terms of trade for the growing country.

### 2. Specific Factors Model (Short Run)
**Total questions using this model: 4** (Practice Final Q2c, PS3 T/F Q3, Q4, PS3 essay indirectly)

**Lectures:** [[Lec9]] (Specific Factors Model — distributional effects, 3 factors, PPF)

**Purpose:** Distributional effects of trade when capital can't move between sectors.

**Factors:** L (mobile between sectors), K (specific to manufacturing M), T (specific to agriculture A).

**Labor market:** PM·MPLM = PA·MPLA = w. Equilibrium allocates labor until wages equalize.

EQUATIONS:

- **Production Function:** $Q_M = Q_M(K, L_M)$ and $Q_A = Q_A(T, L_A)$
    
- **Factor Demand:** $L_i = f(P_i, w, K_i)$
    
- **Market Clearing:** $L_M(P_M, w, K) + L_A(P_A, w, T) = L$


> [!note] Price Increase in M (tariff or trade opening)
> - **Capital in M:** gains unambiguously (price up, wage up less → profit margin expands)
> - **Capital in A:** loses unambiguously (no price change, labor bid away → MPLA falls, wage rises → profit squeezed)
> - **Labor:** ambiguous (real wage rises in A terms, falls in M terms)

> [!important] Key Rule
> Specific factor in protected sector **always gains**. Specific factor in other sector **always loses**. Mobile labor **always ambiguous**.

> [!warning] Common Trap
> Do not confuse with Stolper-Samuelson. SF = short run, industry-specific. S-S = long run, factor-specific. The 1973 data supports SF, not HO/S-S.

**Graph:** Two labor demand curves (PM·MPLM and PA·MPLA) on shared labor axis. Price increase shifts one curve up → labor reallocates → new wage equilibrium.

#### Questions — Specific Factors Model

---

##### [Practice Final] Q2c: Who supports the textile tariff? (Short run)

**Context:** Tariff raises domestic textile price from 60 to 63.

- **Textile capital owners:** Gain unambiguously. Price up, specific factor's return rises.
- **Agricultural capital owners:** Lose unambiguously. No price change, labor bid away.
- **Workers:** Ambiguous. Real wage up in agricultural goods terms, down in textile terms.

---

##### [PS3] T/F Q3: Mobile factors gain more from trade liberalization than immobile factors

> [!warning] FALSE/UNCERTAIN
> Mobile factors gain more than specific factors in comparative disadvantage sectors. But mobile factors gain less than specific factors in comparative advantage sectors. Net outcome depends on sector — no clean directional prediction.

---

##### [PS3] T/F Q4: Specific factors model explains income inequality increases in both developed and developing countries

> [!warning] FALSE
> Developed countries (export skill-intensive goods) → skilled wages rise, unskilled wages fall → rising inequality. ✓ Developing countries (export low-skill goods) → unskilled wages rise, skilled wages fall → falling inequality. Model fits developed world empirically but contradicts observed rising inequality in developing countries.

### 3. Heckscher-Ohlin Model (Long Run)
**Total questions using this model: 5** (Practice Final Q2d, PS3 essay a–c, Final T/F 2, Midterm T/F contrast)

**Lectures:** [[Lec10]] (HO Model, Stolper-Samuelson, Rybczynski, Leontief Paradox) · [[Lec9]] (transition from SF)

**Purpose:** Trade draiven by factor abundance. Two factors (K, L), two goods, two countries, same technology.

> [!note] Core Theorems
> - **HO Theorem:** Capital-abundant country exports capital-intensive good
> - **Stolper-Samuelson:** Price↑ in good X → real return to factor used intensively in X rises, other factor falls — magnified beyond the price change
> - **Factor Price Equalization:** Free trade equalizes factor prices across countries (with same technology)
> - **Rybczynski:** Factor endowment↑ → output of intensive good rises, other falls (at fixed prices)

> [!note] Leontief Paradox (1953)
> U.S. exports were MORE labor-intensive than imports, despite U.S. being capital-abundant → contradicts HO.
> Explanations: human capital, demand bias, natural resources.

> [!tip] Long Run vs Short Run
> HO assumes costless factor mobility across sectors (long run). Specific factors model better fits short run (switching costs high). 1973 congressional data: capital and labor in same industry took same trade position (19/21 industries) → supports specific factors model, not HO.

> [!warning] Common Trap
> S-S is **long run** — all factors mobile across sectors. If exam asks "short run distributional effects," use Specific Factors, not S-S. Tariff on labor-intensive import: ALL workers gain (real w up), ALL capital owners lose (real r down) — no ambiguity unlike SF.

#### Questions — Heckscher-Ohlin / Stolper-Samuelson

---

##### [Practice Final] Q2d: Who supports the textile tariff? (Long run)

**Context:** Textiles are labor-intensive. Tariff raises textile price. Long-run analysis.

- **All workers:** Gain unambiguously. Real w rises (S-S: price↑ in labor-intensive good → real wage rises for all labor).
- **All capital owners:** Lose unambiguously. Real r falls.

> [!tip] Key Contrast with Q2c
> Specific factors → which sector your capital is in determines outcome. Stolper-Samuelson → which factor you own determines outcome, regardless of sector.

---

##### [Practice Final] T/F 2: The Leontief Paradox confirms HO

> [!warning] FALSE
> Leontief (1953): U.S. exports MORE labor-intensive than imports despite U.S. capital abundance → **contradicts** HO. Explanations: human capital, demand bias, natural resources.

##### [PS3] Short Essay: 1973 Congressional Data (parts a, b, c)

**Setup:** 21 industries. Capital and labor trade positions: 14 both protectionist, 5 both free-trade, 1 split each way.

**(a) Does data support HO?**

**NO.** HO predicts winners/losers by factor ownership — all labor vs all capital across industries. Data shows 19/21 industries have capital and labor on the same side → industry membership determines position, not factor type. Supports specific factors model instead.

**(b) HO as a "long run" theory:**

HO assumes costless factor mobility across sectors — realistic only in the long run. In the short run, industry switching is costly → specific factors model more appropriate for observed political behavior.

**(c) Short-run or long-run politics?**

**Short-run.** Industry-based positions (specific factors) dominate factor-based positions (HO). Politicians face election cycles → weight short-run costs of trade highly. People may rationally discount long-run gains if they expect to retire or move before they materialize.

> [!tip] Exam Emphasis
> Industry alignment of capital+labor → specific factors. Factor alignment across industries → HO. 1973 data clearly points to specific factors.

### 4. New Trade Theory (Krugman / IRS Model)
**Total questions using this model: 8** (PS2 Q1 a–h, Practice Midterm Q2 a–e, Midterm T/F 4, PS2 T/F Q1, Q2, Q3)

**Lectures:** [[Lec5]] (NTT Part I — PP/CC curves, autarky + trade equilibrium) · [[Lec6v]] (NTT Part II — empirical evidence, redistributive effects)

**Purpose:** Explain intra-industry trade in similar goods via economies of scale and product variety. No comparative advantage needed.

**Parameters:** F (fixed cost), b = 1/(markup parameter), c (marginal cost), S (market size), n (number of firms/varieties).

> [!note] Core Formulas
> - **PP curve (pricing):** P = c + 1/(n·b) — falls as n rises (more competition → lower markup). Does NOT shift with S.
> - **CC curve (zero profit):** AC = c + F·n/S — rises as n rises. Slope = F/S. Steeper for smaller markets.
> - **Equilibrium:** N\* = √(S/Fb), P\* = √(F/Sb) + c
> - **Trade effect:** S increases → CC shifts right (flatter, lower) along fixed PP → N↑, P↓

> [!tip] Who Gains Most from Trade
> Smaller markets gain more — they had steepest AC curve, furthest from optimal scale. Germany (large) gains least; France/Sweden (small) gain most.

> [!warning] Common Traps
> - Trade shifts **CC** right (not PP) — PP is fixed
> - Larger markets do NOT gain more
> - IRS does NOT predict CA-based exports — intra-industry only
> - IRS cannot explain unemployment (no labor market in model)

**Pandemic / demand shock:** S decreases → CC shifts left (steeper, higher) → N↓, P↑. Fewer varieties, higher prices.

**Intra-industry vs inter-industry trade:** IRS explains intra-industry (same good, different varieties). CA explains inter-industry. Real trade has both → need combined model.

#### Questions — New Trade Theory / IRS

##### [Practice Midterm] Q2: Autos (US + EU)

**Setup:** F = $1.25B, c = $17,000, PP curve: P = 17,000 + 50,000/n. SUS = 900,000, SEU = 1,600,000.

**Autarky equilibria** (b = 1/50,000, Fb = 25,000):

- NUS = √(900,000/25,000) = √36 = **6.** PUS = 17,000 + 50,000/6 ≈ **$25,333.**
- NEU = √(1,600,000/25,000) = √64 = **8.** PEU = 17,000 + 50,000/8 = **$23,250.**

**With trade (S = 2,500,000):**

- NW = √(2,500,000/25,000) = √100 = **10.** PW = 17,000 + 50,000/10 = **$22,000.**
- NUS + NEU = 14 > NW = 10 → **4 firms exit globally.** More variety than either autarky, lower price than either autarky.

**Pandemic (S falls):** CC shifts left and up → N↓, P↑. Fewer varieties, higher prices.

**Defend trade (d):** Two channels: (1) variety-enhancing effect — more models available; (2) pro-competitive effect — lower prices from larger market and more competition.

**Detroit unemployment (e):** Partially explained. Model predicts firms exit (4 exit globally). Workers at exiting firms face real layoffs. But: model has no formal labor market → can't model unemployment directly.

> [!tip] Exam Emphasis
> Know N\* = √(S/Fb). Trade reduces total firm count below pre-trade sum. Two welfare channels: variety + pro-competitive. Pandemic = smaller S = CC shifts adversely.

##### [PS2] Q1: European Autos (4-country IRS)

**Setup:** F = $1B, c = $8,000, PP curve: P = 8,000 + 40,000/N. N = √(S/25,000).

**Autarky results:**

| Country | S | N | Q = S/N | P |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Germany | 625,000 | 5 | 125,000 | $16,000 |
| Italy | 400,000 | 4 | 100,000 | $18,000 |
| France | 100,000 | 2 | 50,000 | $28,000 |
| Sweden | 100,000 | 2 | 50,000 | $28,000 |
| **World** | **1,225,000** | **7** | **175,000** | **$13,714** |

**Free trade:** S = 1,225,000. N = 7. P ≈ **$13,714.** Sum of autarky N = 13 → **6 firms exit globally.**

**AC curve slopes (= F/S):** France/Sweden = 10,000 (steepest), Italy = 2,500, Germany = 1,600, Integrated = 816 (flattest).

**(f)(i) Consumer benefit:** More varieties (N rises) + lower price (from $28K to $13.7K for France/Sweden).

**(f)(ii) Most gain:** France and Sweden — largest price drop, largest variety gain.

**(f)(iii) Least gain:** Germany — already lowest autarky price, closest to world equilibrium.

**(f)(iv) Why:** Smaller markets → steeper AC → further from optimal scale → larger gains from integration.

**(g) Distributional effects:** Workers/managers at exiting firms face adjustment costs. Owners of firm-specific capital lose. Expanding firm workers gain. IRS assumes costless reallocation — unrealistic short run.

**(h) France produces few cars despite large market:** Need combined model — IRS explains why France exports some varieties, but CA/HO explains why France is net importer (comparative disadvantage in autos). IRS alone can't explain net import position.

> [!tip] Exam Emphasis
> N = √(S/Fb). Slope of AC = F/S. Smaller S → steeper AC → more to gain from trade. World firm count < sum of national counts → distributional consequences.

##### [PS2] T/F Q1: IRS model predicts countries export varieties in which they have comparative advantage

> [!warning] FALSE
> IRS/monopolistic competition makes no prediction based on CA. It predicts both imports and exports of the same good in different varieties regardless of CA. Need combined IRS + HO model for both inter- and intra-industry predictions.

##### [PS2] T/F Q2: Countries with larger domestic markets benefit more from trade

> [!warning] FALSE
> Larger markets already have lower prices and more variety in autarky. Smaller markets gain most — steepest AC curve, furthest from optimal scale. Trade brings them to world equilibrium with dramatically more variety and lower prices.

##### [PS2] T/F Q3: Intra-industry trade must be driven by economies of scale rather than comparative advantage

> [!note] TRUE
> Within-industry trade in differentiated varieties is explained by IRS + monopolistic competition. CA theories explain inter-industry trade (different goods). US-Mexico computer parts trade (same HS code, both ways) = intra-industry = IRS explanation.

##### [Practice Midterm] T/F 4: Intra-industry trade between US and Mexico better explained by economies of scale than CA

> [!note] TRUE
> CA (Ricardian, HO) explains inter-industry trade. Intra-industry trade (same good, different varieties) arises from product differentiation + economies of scale. Arises even between similar countries. Need combined model for both.

### 5. Trade Policy — Small Economy
**Total questions using this model: 4** (PS4 Q1 a–d, PS4 T/F Q2, Practice Final T/F 3 indirectly)

**Lectures:** [[Lec11]] (Trade Policy Part I — tariffs, history, U.S.-China trade war) · [[Lec12]]

**Purpose:** Tariff/quota welfare when country can't affect world price.

> [!note] Core Mechanics
> - **Tariff t:** Domestic price = PW + t. QS↑, QD↓, imports↓. CS falls, PS rises, government revenue = t × M. Net = −DWL (two triangles).
> - **DWL triangles:** Production distortion = 0.5 × ΔQS × t. Consumption distortion = 0.5 × ΔQD × t.
> - **Optimal tariff (small economy) = 0.** Any tariff creates pure DWL.

> [!note] Quota vs Tariff
> Set quota = M at tariff price → same price/quantity. Difference: revenue goes to quota license holder. If foreign firm holds license → rent transferred abroad → domestic welfare lower than equivalent tariff.
>
> **Tariff ≡ Quota iff:** same import volume AND domestic entity holds quota rents.
>
> **Under uncertainty:** Tariff fixes price (quantity adjusts); quota fixes quantity (price adjusts).

> [!warning] Common Trap
> Small economy NEVER has a ToT gain — world price is fixed. Optimal tariff is always zero.

### 6. Trade Policy — Large Economy
**Total questions using this model: 5** (PS4 Q2 a–d, Practice Final Q2a, Q2b, PS4 T/F Q1, Practice Final T/F 3)

**Lectures:** [[Lec11]] (Trade Policy Part I) · [[Lec13]] (Trade Policy Part III — optimal tariffs, import quotas, VERs)

**Purpose:** Large country can improve terms of trade; optimal tariff > 0.

M = QD - QS

IM = EX*

PD = PF + t

PF = PD - t

EX_new = EX_(PD - t)

IM = EX*new

ToT = (PW - PF) * New Imports

Production DWL = 0.5 * (New QS - Old QS) * (PD - PW)

Consumption DWL = 0.5 * (Old QD - New QD) * (PD - PW)

Net Welfare = ToT - (Production DWL + Consumption DWL)

Equivalent Quota = New Imports


> [!note] Core Mechanics
> - **Tariff mechanics:** Import demand shifts down by t → foreign export price PF falls below PW. Domestic price rises by less than t.
> - **Finding equilibrium:** Set M(PD) = EX\*(PF), with PD = PF + t. Solve for PD and PF simultaneously.
> - **ToT gain:** (PW − PF) × M = reduction in import price × import volume
> - **DWL:** Same two triangles as small economy (based on PD vs PW)
> - **Net welfare:** ToT gain − DWL. Optimal t\* = 1/ε\* where ε\* = elasticity of foreign export supply.

> [!warning] Common Traps
> - Must find PF separately using foreign export supply — PF ≠ PW − t
> - Large economy can still have negative net welfare if DWL > ToT gain
> - Foreign retaliation → trade war → both lose

### 7. Offshoring / FDI
**Total questions using this model: 9** (PS3 Q1 a–e, Practice Final Q1 a–e, PS3 T/F Q1, Q2, Practice Final T/F 1)

**Lectures:** [[Lec7]] (FDI, Multinational Firms — horizontal vs. vertical FDI, Nestlé, Nike, Intel) · [[Lec13]] (policy context) · `mne_class_activity.pdf`

**Purpose:** Firms minimize total cost across all location combinations for each production step.

> [!note] Core Method
> Enumerate all combos (each step in US or China). Total cost = Σ(unit labor cost × labor req) + shipping between steps. Choose minimum.
>
> **Shipping:** Only pay shipping if steps are in different countries. Assembled goods cost more to ship than components.

> [!note] FDI Types
> - **Horizontal FDI:** Replicate full production in foreign country to serve local market. Brainard (1997): preferred when trade costs HIGH, plant-level scale economies LOW.
> - **Vertical FDI:** Fragment production — place steps in cheapest location. Profitable if: VC savings × Q > fixed setup cost.
> - **Export-platform FDI:** Produce in low-cost country (China), ship to third market (Europe).
> - **Mixed strategy:** Components in cheapest location, assembly in served market.

> [!tip] 4 Key Determinants of FDI
> (1) Transportation costs · (2) Cross-country wage differences · (3) Fixed costs of setting up plant · (4) Market size

> [!warning] Common Trap
> Multinationals are NOT primarily in labor-intensive industries — they dominate skill-intensive, capital-intensive, R&D-intensive sectors (machinery, pharma, vehicles).

**Fixed cost threshold:** Min Q = F / (VC_old − VC_new)
#### Equations

##### 1. Horizontal FDI (Market-Seeking)

Horizontal FDI occurs when a firm replicates its **entire production process** in a foreign country.

- **Definition:** The firm builds a "mirror image" plant abroad to produce the same goods that it produces at home.
    
- **Motivation:** It is driven by **proximity incentives**. By producing locally, the firm avoids trade costs (like transportation fees or tariffs) and gets closer to foreign consumers, allowing them to adapt to local tastes or market conditions.
    
- **Key Example:** A U.S.-based car manufacturer building a factory in Germany to sell cars directly to German consumers, avoiding the costs of shipping completed vehicles across the Atlantic.
    

##### 2. Vertical FDI (Efficiency-Seeking)

Vertical FDI occurs when a firm fragments its production chain and places different stages of production in different countries based on their comparative advantage.

- **Definition:** The firm breaks the production process into stages (e.g., R&D, component manufacturing, final assembly) and locates each stage where it is most cost-efficient.
    
- **Motivation:** It is driven by **efficiency or input cost incentives**. A firm might conduct R&D in a high-skill, capital-abundant country and move labor-intensive assembly to a low-wage country.
    
- **Key Example:** The "Barbie Doll" example from your lecture:
    
    - **R&D and Molds:** United States (High-skill)
        
    - **Raw Materials:** Taiwan and Japan
        
    - **Assembly:** Indonesia, Malaysia, or China (Low-wage, labor-intensive)

##### [Practice Final / PS3] Q1: Computers (US vs China)

**Setup:** US firm, two steps (components + assembly), US vs China.
Wages: wUS_unskilled = $10, wUS_skilled = $20, wCHN_unskilled = $2, wCHN_skilled = $10.

| | US Components | US Assembly | CHN Components | CHN Assembly |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unskilled hrs | 10 | 10 | 20 | 40 |
| Skilled hrs | 5 | 2 | 10 | 4 |
| **Cost** | **$200** | **$140** | **$140** | **$120** |

Shipping: $10 (components), $50 (assembled).

**Part (a) — No shipping costs:** All China = $140 + $120 = **$260.** Cheapest.

**Part (b) — With shipping costs:** All combos:

- All US: $200 + $140 = $340
- All China + ship assembled: $140 + $120 + $50 = $310
- CHN components → US assembly: $140 + $10 + $140 = **$290** ← cheapest
- US components → CHN assembly: $200 + $10 + $120 = $330

**Optimal: CHN components, US assembly = $290. Vertical FDI.**

**Part (c) — Fixed cost $50M:**
VC savings = $340 − $290 = $50/computer. Min Q = $50M / $50 = **1,000,000 units.**

**Part (d) — Chinese market (600K units at $360):**

- Export from US: $200 + $140 + $50 = $390 > $360 → not viable.
- All China: profit = 600K × ($360 − $260) − $50M = $60M − $50M = **$10M > 0** → set up China plant.
- China market alone covers fixed cost → min US sales to justify plant = **0. Mixed horizontal + vertical FDI.**

**Part (e) — European market (500K or 3M):**

- Export from China: $310/unit. Set up EU plant (CHN components + EU assembly): $290/unit − $50M fixed.
- At 500K: EU plant profit lower by $40M → **export from China.**
- At 3M: EU plant beats export by $10M → **export-platform FDI: CHN components → EU assembly.**

> [!tip] Exam Emphasis
> Enumerate all combos. Add shipping where steps cross borders. Fixed cost threshold = F / (VC_old − VC_new). Know the three FDI types: vertical (fragment by step), horizontal (replicate for local market), export-platform (produce in third country to serve market).

### 8. Strategic Trade Policy / Game Theory
**Total questions using this model: 2** (Practice Final Part II steps 1–3)

**Lectures:** [[Lec13]] (Trade Policy Part III) · [[Lec14]] (Strategic Trade Policy, Political Economy)

**Purpose:** When oligopoly rents exist, government subsidy can shift profits to domestic firms.

> [!note] Core Steps
> 1. **Nash equilibrium:** No player unilaterally deviates. Check each cell: is each firm's strategy a best response to the other's?
> 2. **Dominant strategy:** Best response regardless of opponent's action.
> 3. **Subsidy effect:** Add x to domestic firm's payoffs in "Produce" row. Converts situation where domestic firm wouldn't produce into domestic firm always producing.
> 4. **Minimum subsidy:** Find x such that domestic payoff when both produce + x > 0.
> 5. **Net welfare:** If profit extracted from foreign firm > subsidy cost → net gain.

> [!warning] Common Traps
> - Subsidy adds x to **Produce row only** — not all rows
> - Check all 4 cells for Nash equilibrium — don't assume dominant strategy exists
> - Risk of retaliation makes net gain uncertain

### 9. Political Economy of Trade
**Total questions using this model: 4** (Practice Final Q2e, PS4 T/F Q3, PS3 essay part c, Midterm context)

**Lectures:** [[Lec14]] (Political Economy — median voter, collective action, "protection for sale")

**Purpose:** Why protection persists despite aggregate welfare loss.

> [!note] Core Arguments
> - **Collective action problem:** Producers (concentrated, organized) lobby effectively. Consumers (diffuse gains) don't. Industry-level politics dominate factor-level politics.
> - **Median voter theorem:** Predicts free trade (majority gains). Fails empirically — concentrated interests dominate.
> - **Short run vs long run politics:** Politicians face election cycles → weight short-run effects more. HO (long run) predictions less politically relevant than specific factors (short run) predictions.

> [!tip] 1973 Congressional Data (21 industries)
> 19/21 industries had capital and labor taking the same side. 14 anti-trade, 5 pro-trade.
> → Supports **specific factors model** (industry-based, short-run), NOT HO (factor-based, long-run).

> [!note] Gravity Model
> Trade_ij ∝ (GDP_i × GDP_j) / Distance_ij. Explains why Sweden-Germany trade > Sweden-Spain trade (Germany larger GDP, closer).
