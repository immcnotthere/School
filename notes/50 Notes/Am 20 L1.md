## Why Differential Equations?
 
> [!quote] The Big Idea
> Science is roughly **the intersection of theory and data**.
> Theory, being limited in what it can say about the world absolutely, most often finds its home not in *how things are* — but in **how things change**.
> e.g. Price Law, Supply and Demand.
 
**Derivatives** are how we calculate change in mathematics. Recall: *calculus* was born as the study of how to find the rate at which things change.
 
$$\text{behave} \implies \text{change} \implies \text{derivatives}$$
 
### The Modeling Cycle
 
$$\text{Theoretical Assumptions} \xrightarrow{\text{Mathematical Methods}} \text{Mathematical Model} \xrightarrow{\text{compare}} \text{Observational Data}$$
 
A **[[Mathematical model]]** is one or more equations that describe the relations between variables in a theory — used across physics, biology, medicine, chemistry, engineering, etc.

## Definitions
 
### Vocab 1 — Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE)
 
> [!definition] ODE
> An **ordinary differential equation (ODE)** is an equation of the form
> $$F\!\left(x,\, y,\, y',\, y'',\, \ldots,\, y^{(n)}\right) = 0$$
> where $y$ is a function of the single variable $x$.
 
> [!note] Why "ordinary"?
> It is called *[[Ordinary (Math)]]* because $y$ is a function of **one** variable. This is **not** a course on partial differentiation (PDEs).
 
> [!warning] Heads up
> This general form $F(\ldots) = 0$ is **not** how ODEs typically appear in context, nor how they are solved. It is also not the standard way we write differential equations in practice.
 
---
 
### Vocab 2 — Order
 
> [!definition] Order
> The **order** of an ODE is the order of the **highest derivative** appearing in the equation.
 
**Examples:**
 
| Equation | Order |
|---|---|
| $y''' + 2y' = x^2 - 1$ | 3rd order |
| $y'' + y = \sin t$ | 2nd order |
 
---
 
### Vocab 3 — Normal Form
 
> [!definition] Normal Form
> An ODE is in **normal form** if the highest derivative is expressed as a function of the remaining derivatives and the independent variable:
> $$y^{(n)} = f\!\left(x,\, y,\, y',\, \ldots,\, y^{(n-1)}\right)$$

%%
This form is important when we want to prove that certain ODEs have solutions or are unique. In most cases we will however move away from this form because other formats are much easier to understand. 
%%

**Example:**
$$y'' = ay' + f(t)$$
 
---
 
### Vocab 4 — Initial Value Problem (IVP)
 
> [!definition] IVP
> An **initial value problem (IVP)** is an ODE together with **prescribed values** at a specific point for ==**all but the highest derivative**==:
> $$F\!\left(x,\, y,\, y',\, y'',\, \ldots,\, y^{(n)}\right) = 0$$
> $$y(x_0) = y_0,\quad y'(x_0) = y_1,\quad \ldots,\quad y^{(n-1)}(x_0) = y_{n-1}$$

%%

**Solution of an IVP:** A function $y(t)$ defined and $n$-times differentiable on an open interval $(a, b)$ containing $x_0$, such that:
1. $F\!\left(x,\, y,\, y',\, \ldots,\, y^{(n)}\right) = 0$ holds for every $x \in (a,b)$
2. $y$ satisfies all the prescribed initial values at $x_0$

**TLDR:** Because derivatives are limits, the solution must exist in a "neighborhood" around $x_0$. We need an open interval so we can approach $x_0$ from both sides, ensuring the derivatives in the ODE are well-defined at the initial point.

### Example 2 — Newton's Law of Cooling / Warming
 
> A body is immersed in a medium (air, water, …) of constant temperature $T_m$.

Newton says in this situation that...
 
>The temperature $T$ of the body changes at a rate **proportional to the difference** $T - T_m$:
 
$$\frac{dT}{dt} = k(T - T_m), \qquad k = \text{constant}$$
 
**Solution:**
 
$$T(t) = T_m + (T_0 - T_m)\,e^{kt}$$
%%
The math to get there
$$\frac{dT}{dt} = k(T - T_m)$$

$$\frac{1}{T - T_m} dT = k \, dt$$

$$\int \frac{1}{T - T_m} dT = \int k \, dt$$

$$\ln|T - T_m| = kt + C$$

$$|T - T_m| = e^{kt + C}$$

$$T - T_m = \pm e^C e^{kt}$$

$$T(t) = T_m + A e^{kt}$$

$$T(0) = T_0 \implies T_0 = T_m + A e^{k(0)}$$

$$T_0 = T_m + A$$

$$A = T_0 - T_m$$

$$T(t) = T_m + (T_0 - T_m)e^{kt}$$

%%
> [!note]
> Observe: as $t \to \infty$, if $k < 0$, then $T(t) \to T_m$ — the body equilibrates to the ambient temperature.

### Example 3 — Lotka–Volterra Predator–Prey Model
 
$$\begin{cases} x' = ax - bxy \\[4pt] y' = -cy + dxy \end{cases} \qquad a,b,c,d > 0$$
 
where:
- $x(t)$ = population size of the **prey** species
- $y(t)$ = population size of the **predator** species
**Comments:**
- We learn very easily here that a critical feature of a system of dif. equations is that they must interact
- In reality it tends to be pretty accurate
- So what happens is that we need that interaction such that
-  The $xy$ terms model **interaction** between species.
- With no predators ($y = 0$): prey grows exponentially ($x' = ax$).
- With no prey ($x = 0$): predators decline exponentially ($y' = -cy$).
This is a **system** of two coupled first-order ODEs — and is **nonlinear**.

### Example 4 — Undamped Pendulum
 
**Setup:**
- $L$ = length of pendulum
- $m$ = mass of bob
- $s(t)$ = arc-length position at time $t$
- $\theta(t)$ = angle at time $t$
- $F_T$ = tangential component of gravity; $F_R$ = radial component (what moves it back and forth)
**Derivation via Newton's Second Law:**
 
$$m\,s'' = F_T = -mg\sin\theta$$
 
Since $s = L\theta \implies s'' = L\theta''$:
 
$$mL\theta'' = -mg\sin\theta$$
 
$$\boxed{\theta'' + \frac{g}{L}\sin\theta = 0}$$
 
This is a **2nd-order nonlinear ODE**.

### Example 5 — Spring–Mass–Dashpot System
 
Three forces act on mass $m$:
 
| Force | Expression |
|-------|-----------|
| Spring (restoring) | $-by$ |
| Dashpot (damping, resists motion) | $-ay'$ |
| External forcing | $f(t)$ |
 
By Newton's Second Law ($ma = \sum F$):
 
$$m\,y'' = -ay' - by + f(t)$$
 
$$\boxed{m\,y'' + a\,y' + b\,y = f(t)} \qquad m,a,b > 0$$
 
This is also the equation governing **current in an RLC circuit**.

## Linear vs. Nonlinear ODEs
 
### Vocab 5 — Linear ODE
 
> [!definition] Linear ODE
> An ODE is **linear** if it has the form
> $$a_n(t)\,y^{(n)} + a_{n-1}(t)\,y^{(n-1)} + \cdots + a_1(t)\,y' + a_0(t)\,y = f(t)$$
> The equation is linear **in** $y,\, y',\, \ldots,\, y^{(n)}$.
> The function $f(t)$ is called the **forcing function**.
 
### Examples by Order
 
**First-order linear:**
 
| Equation | Phenomenon |
|---|---|
| $y' - ay = 0$ | Exponential growth/decay |
| $y' + ky = 0$ | Newton's law of cooling/warming (if $k > 0$) |
 
**Second-order linear:**
 
$$m\,y'' + a\,y' + b\,y = f(t) \qquad (m,a,b > 0)$$
 
Models: spring-mass system, RLC circuit.
 
**Nonlinear:**
 
$$\theta'' + \frac{g}{L}\sin\theta = 0$$
 
This is **nonlinear** because of the $\sin\theta$ term — it cannot be written as a linear combination of $\theta$ and its derivatives.
 
> [!tip] Linearization
> For small angles, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, so the pendulum equation becomes
> $$\theta'' + \frac{g}{L}\,\theta = 0$$
> which **is** linear — this is the simple harmonic oscillator.
