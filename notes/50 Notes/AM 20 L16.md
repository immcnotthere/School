## Overview

This lecture builds differential equation models for three physical scenarios involving a mass on a spring:

1. **Part A** — Only the spring acts (undamped, unforced → simple harmonic motion)
2. **Part B** — An external periodic force drives the system (beats, resonance)
3. **Part C** — Friction or a dashpot removes energy (damped, decaying motion)

> [!tip] Physical intuition Pull a weight on a spring and let go → it oscillates naturally. Push it periodically → the motion changes. Add fluid resistance → the oscillation dies out over time.

## Part A — Unforced, Undamped Vibrations

### Slide 1–2 · [[Hooke's Law]] & Equilibrium

**Setup:** A mass $m$ hangs from a spring with natural length $L$. Gravity stretches the spring by $\Delta L$ until the system reaches [[Equilibrium (Math)]].

**Hooke's Law** — the spring exerts a restoring force proportional to stretch:

$$F_s = k,\Delta L$$

where $k$ is the **spring constant** (stiffness, units: force/length).

**Equilibrium condition** — upward spring force balances downward gravity:

$$k,\Delta{L} - mg = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad mg = k,\Delta L$$

> [!note] Key insight This equilibrium relationship is practically useful: if you observe how much the spring stretches under a known weight, you can compute $k/m$ directly without measuring $k$ and $m$ separately.

- Example 1:  The "Metric" Mystery Mass
	- The Scenario: You hang a mystery object on a spring. You don't have a scale to weigh it, and the spring has no labels. You measure that the spring stretches 10 cm ($0.1$ meters) just to hold the weight still.
	- The Goal: Find the natural frequency ($\omega_0$) so you can predict the bounce.
		- Identify the sag ($\Delta L$): $0.1$ m.
		- Use standard gravity ($g$): $9.8$ m/s².
		- Apply the Insight:$$\frac{k}{m} = \frac{g}{\Delta L} = \frac{9.8}{0.1} = 98$$
		- Find the bounce speed:$$\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{98} \approx 9.9 \text{ rad/sec}$$
	- The "So-What": It doesn't matter if that was a heavy lead brick or a light wooden block. Any object that causes a 10 cm sag on its respective spring will bounce at exactly $9.9$ rad/sec.

So simply put:
> **Newton’s Second Law states that mass times acceleration ($m\times y''$) equals the sum of the gravitational and spring forces: $my'' = -mg + k(\Delta L - y)$.** 

>**Because gravity and the spring's initial stretch cancel each other out at equilibrium ($mg = k\Delta L$), the equation simplifies to $$my'' + ky = 0$$**

This clearly has no additional scaling in either direction so we can say the spring is:

>this the ODE that describes the motion of an undamped, unforced spring-mass system.

---

This differential equation proves that an undamped, unforced system will move in a perfect, repeating cycle forever.

- $y > 0$ → mass is **above** equilibrium
- $y < 0$ → mass is **below** equilibrium
- $y = 0$ → equilibrium (rest position)

Using this variable removes gravity from the equation (the constant terms cancel later).


> [!note] If the mass moves down ($y < 0$), the spring stretches further and pulls harder upward — that restoring effect is what causes oscillation.

### Slide 5 · Deriving the ODE

Apply **Newton's Second Law** ($F = ma$, with $a = y''$):

$$my'' = -mg + k(\Delta L - y)$$

Expand the right side:

$$my'' = -mg + k,\Delta L - ky$$

Since equilibrium gives $mg = k,\Delta L$, those two terms cancel:

$$my'' = -ky$$

$$\boxed{my'' + ky = 0}$$

This is the ODE for **unforced, undamped** spring-mass motion.

> [!tip] Why the constants cancel Measuring displacement from equilibrium (rather than from the spring's natural length) is precisely what makes $mg$ and $k\Delta L$ cancel — it's a clever coordinate choice that simplifies everything.

### Slide 6 · Solving the ODE — Natural Frequency

**Characteristic equation:** (See [[AM20 L..]])


# BREAK - See Lecture intro. characteristics



$$mr^2 + k = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad r = \pm,i\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$$

The roots are purely imaginary, giving oscillatory (sine/cosine) solutions with no growth or decay.

**Define the natural frequency:**

$$\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$$

**General solution:**

$$y = c_1\cos(\omega_0 t) + c_2\sin(\omega_0 t)$$

**Period** (time for one full oscillation):

$$T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0}$$

> [!note] The natural frequency $\omega_0$ depends only on the spring stiffness $k$ and the mass $m$ — not on how hard you pull or the starting position. This is a remarkable property of simple harmonic motion.

### Slide 7–9 · Example 1 — Solving an IVP

**Problem:** A 10 g mass stretches a spring 5 cm at equilibrium. The mass is pulled **down** 6 cm and given an initial **upward** velocity of 7 cm/s. Find the equation of motion.

**Step 1 — Choose units (CGS: cm, g, s)**

$$g = 980;\text{cm/s}^2$$

**Step 2 — Find $k/m$ from equilibrium**

$$k,\Delta L = mg \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{k}{m} = \frac{g}{\Delta L} = \frac{980}{5} = 196$$

So $\omega_0^2 = 196$ and $\omega_0 = 14$ rad/s.

**Step 3 — Write the ODE**

$$y'' + 196y = 0$$

**Step 4 — General solution**

$$y = c_1\cos(14t) + c_2\sin(14t)$$

**Step 5 — Apply initial conditions**

The mass starts 6 cm _below_ equilibrium (negative direction) and moves upward:

$$y(0) = -6 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad c_1 = -6$$

$$y'(t) = -14c_1\sin(14t) + 14c_2\cos(14t)$$

$$y'(0) = 7 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad 14c_2 = 7 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad c_2 = \tfrac{1}{2}$$

**Solution:**

$$\boxed{y = -6\cos(14t) + \tfrac{1}{2}\sin(14t)}$$

> [!example] Graph description (Slide 10) **Function plotted:** $y = -6\cos(14t) + \frac{1}{2}\sin(14t)$
> 
> - Starts at $y = -6$ (below equilibrium)
> - Oscillates smoothly between approximately $+6$ and $-6$
> - Period: $T = 2\pi/14 \approx 0.449$ s
> - **Amplitude is constant** — no energy loss, mass swings forever
> - The graph shows roughly 2 full cycles over $t \in [0, 0.75]$

### Slide 11–13 · Amplitude–Phase Form

Any solution $y = c_1\cos(\omega_0 t) + c_2\sin(\omega_0 t)$ can be rewritten as a single cosine with a phase shift:

$$y = R\cos(\omega_0 t - \phi)$$

**Using the identity:**

$$\cos(\alpha - \beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta + \sin\alpha\sin\beta$$

we match:

$$R\cos\phi = c_1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad R\sin\phi = c_2$$

**Finding $R$ (amplitude)** — square and add both equations:

$$R^2 = c_1^2 + c_2^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad R = \sqrt{c_1^2 + c_2^2}$$

For Example 1: $R = \sqrt{(-6)^2 + (1/2)^2} = \sqrt{36.25} \approx 6.02$ cm

**Finding $\phi$ (phase angle):**

$$\cos\phi = \frac{c_1}{R} \approx \frac{-6}{6.02} \approx -0.997$$

$$\sin\phi = \frac{c_2}{R} \approx \frac{0.5}{6.02} \approx 0.083$$

Since $\cos\phi < 0$ and $\sin\phi > 0$, the angle is in **Quadrant II**:

$$\phi \approx 3.058 \text{ radians}$$

**Final amplitude–phase form:**

$$\boxed{y = 6.02\cos(14t - 3.058)}$$

> [!tip] What does the phase angle tell you? The phase angle $\phi$ encodes _where in its cycle_ the oscillation starts. It's a time shift — the motion looks exactly the same as a cosine, just delayed by $\phi/\omega_0$ seconds.

---

### Slide 14 · Simple Harmonic Motion — General Summary

Any equation of the form

$$y'' + \omega_0^2,y = 0$$

produces **simple harmonic motion** with solution:

$$y = c_1\cos(\omega_0 t) + c_2\sin(\omega_0 t) = R\cos(\omega_0 t - \phi)$$

where:

$$R = \sqrt{c_1^2 + c_2^2}, \qquad \cos\phi = \frac{c_1}{R}, \qquad \sin\phi = \frac{c_2}{R}$$

Simple harmonic motion appears throughout mechanics, electrical circuits, acoustics, waves, and quantum physics — whenever there is a restoring force proportional to displacement.

## Part B — Forced, Undamped Vibrations

### Slide 15–16 · Adding an External Force

Now include a periodic external force $f(t)$:

$$my'' + ky = f(t)$$

Focus on sinusoidal forcing at frequency $\omega$:

$$f(t) = F_0\cos(\omega t) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad my'' + ky = F_0\cos(\omega t)$$

Dividing through by $m$:

$$y'' + \omega_0^2,y = \frac{F_0}{m}\cos(\omega t)$$

**Case 1: $\omega \neq \omega_0$** (forcing frequency ≠ natural frequency)

Try a particular solution of the form $y_p = A\cos(\omega t)$.

---

### Slide 17 · Finding the Particular Solution

Substitute $y_p = A\cos(\omega t)$ into the ODE:

$$y_p'' + \omega_0^2,y_p = -A\omega^2\cos(\omega t) + A\omega_0^2\cos(\omega t) = A(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)\cos(\omega t)$$

Setting equal to the right-hand side:

$$A(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)\cos(\omega t) = \frac{F_0}{m}\cos(\omega t)$$

$$\boxed{A = \frac{F_0}{m(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)}}$$

> [!warning] Resonance danger As $\omega \to \omega_0$, the denominator $\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 \to 0$, so $A \to \infty$. Even a tiny force can cause enormous oscillations near resonance.

---

### Slide 18 · General Forced Solution & Beats

The full general solution (particular + homogeneous):

$$y = \frac{F_0}{m(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)}\cos(\omega t) + c_1\cos(\omega_0 t) + c_2\sin(\omega_0 t)$$

Applying **rest initial conditions** $y(0) = y'(0) = 0$:

$$c_1 = -\frac{F_0}{m(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)}, \qquad c_2 = 0$$

**Final form:**

$$\boxed{y = \frac{F_0}{m(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)}\bigl(\cos(\omega t) - \cos(\omega_0 t)\bigr)}$$

The motion is a superposition of natural oscillation and forced oscillation. Their interaction creates **beats**.

---

### Slide 19 · Trig Identity for Beats

Use the identity:

$$\cos(\alpha - \beta) - \cos(\alpha + \beta) = 2\sin\alpha\sin\beta$$

Write $\omega t = \alpha - \beta$ and $\omega_0 t = \alpha + \beta$, so:

$$\alpha = \frac{\omega_0 + \omega}{2}t, \qquad \beta = \frac{\omega_0 - \omega}{2}t$$

Therefore:

$$\cos(\omega t) - \cos(\omega_0 t) = 2\sin!\left(\frac{\omega_0 + \omega}{2}t\right)\sin!\left(\frac{\omega_0 - \omega}{2}t\right)$$

---

### Slide 20 · Beat Phenomenon

Substituting the identity:

$$\boxed{y = \frac{2F_0}{m(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)}\sin!\left(\frac{\omega_0 - \omega}{2}t\right)\sin!\left(\frac{\omega_0 + \omega}{2}t\right)}$$

**Terminology:**

|Term|Expression|Role|
|---|---|---|
|**Carrier wave**|$\displaystyle\sin!\left(\frac{\omega_0+\omega}{2}t\right)$|Fast oscillation|
|**Envelope** $R(t)$|$\displaystyle\frac{2F_0}{m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)}\sin!\left(\frac{\omega_0-\omega}{2}t\right)$|Slow amplitude modulation|

> [!note] What beats sound and look like The fast carrier wave oscillates rapidly while $R(t)$ slowly grows and shrinks. You hear this in music as a pulsing "wah-wah" when two strings are slightly out of tune.

---

### Slide 21 · Near Resonance

When $|\omega_0 - \omega|$ is very small (frequencies nearly equal):

$$\max|R(t)| = \frac{2F_0}{m|\omega_0^2 - \omega^2|}$$

becomes **very large**. Even a small driving force $F_0$ can cause enormous oscillations if $\omega \approx \omega_0$.

---

### Slide 22 · Example 2 — Beats

**Given:** $k = 400$, $m = 4$, $f(t) = 150\cos(8t)$, initial rest.

$$\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{100} = 10, \qquad \omega = 8, \qquad \frac{F_0}{m} = 37.5$$

$$y'' + 100y = 37.5\cos(8t), \qquad y(0) = y'(0) = 0$$

Applying the beats formula:

$$y = \frac{2F_0}{m(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)}\sin!\left(\frac{\omega_0 - \omega}{2}t\right)\sin!\left(\frac{\omega_0 + \omega}{2}t\right)$$

$$= \frac{75}{36}\sin(t)\sin(9t)$$

> [!example] Graph description (Slide 22) **Three curves shown:**
> 
> - **Blue solid line:** $y = \frac{75}{36}\sin(t)\sin(9t)$ — the actual motion; fast oscillation with slowly varying amplitude
> - **Orange dashed:** $R(t) = \frac{75}{36}\sin(t)$ — the upper envelope, a slow sine wave of period $\approx 6.3$
> - **Green dashed:** $-R(t) = -\frac{75}{36}\sin(t)$ — the lower envelope
> - The blue curve bounces rapidly inside the dashed envelope, which itself oscillates between about $\pm 2.1$
> - This is the classic **beat pattern** — bursts of oscillation separated by near-silence

---

### Slide 23 · Near-Resonance Example

**Given:** $k = 400$, $m = 4$, $f(t) = 0.5\cos(9.999t)$, initial rest.

$$\omega_0 = 10, \qquad \omega = 9.999, \qquad \frac{2F_0}{m|\omega_0^2 - \omega^2|} = \frac{4}{4 \times 0.02} = 50$$

Although $F_0$ is very small (forcing amplitude = 0.5), the near-resonant response has maximum amplitude **50** — 100× the forcing size.

---

### Slide 24 (Case 2) · Resonance: $\omega = \omega_0$

When the forcing frequency exactly matches the natural frequency, the particular solution form $A\cos(\omega_0 t)$ fails (resonance). Use **reduction of order** and try:

$$y_p = At\sin(\omega_0 t)$$

Compute derivatives:

$$y_p' = A\sin(\omega_0 t) + A\omega_0 t\cos(\omega_0 t)$$

$$y_p'' = 2A\omega_0\cos(\omega_0 t) - A\omega_0^2 t\sin(\omega_0 t)$$

Substitute into $y'' + \omega_0^2 y = \frac{F_0}{m}\cos(\omega_0 t)$:

$$2A\omega_0\cos(\omega_0 t) = \frac{F_0}{m}\cos(\omega_0 t)$$

$$A = \frac{F_0}{2m\omega_0}$$

$$\boxed{y_p = \frac{F_0}{2m\omega_0},t\sin(\omega_0 t)}$$

> [!warning] Resonance = unbounded growth The factor of $t$ out front means amplitude grows **linearly without bound**. Each push adds energy at exactly the right moment — like pushing a swing in perfect sync. In reality, something breaks first.

> [!example] Graph description (Slide 28) **Function plotted:** $y_p = \frac{F_0}{2m\omega_0},t\sin(\omega_0 t)$
> 
> - The solution oscillates at frequency $\omega_0$ (carrier: $\sin(\omega_0 t)$)
> - But the amplitude **grows linearly** — the envelope is $\pm\frac{F_0}{2m\omega_0}t$, shown as dashed diagonal lines widening like a triangle
> - Starting at zero amplitude, the oscillations become progressively larger over time — unbounded growth

## Part C — Damped Vibrations

### Slide 29–30 · Spring–Mass–Dashpot System

Add a **dashpot** (viscous damper) producing a force proportional to velocity:

$$F_{\text{damp}} = -cy'$$

Newton's Second Law now gives:

$$my'' = -cy' + k(\Delta L - y) - mg$$

Using $mg = k,\Delta L$, the constant terms cancel again:

$$\boxed{my'' + cy' + ky = 0}$$

**Parameters:**

|Symbol|Meaning|
|---|---|
|$m$|Mass|
|$c > 0$|Damping constant|
|$k > 0$|Spring constant|

> [!note] The damping term $cy'$ removes energy from the system. The larger $c$, the more friction, and the faster oscillations die out.

---

### Slide 31–32 · Characteristic Equation & Overdamping

**Characteristic equation:**

$$mr^2 + cr + k = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad r = \frac{-c \pm \sqrt{c^2 - 4mk}}{2m}$$

**Three cases based on the discriminant $c^2 - 4mk$:**

|Discriminant|Roots|Behavior|
|---|---|---|
|$c^2 - 4mk > 0$|Two real, both negative|Overdamped — no oscillation|
|$c^2 - 4mk = 0$|One repeated real root|Critically damped|
|$c^2 - 4mk < 0$|Complex conjugate pair|Underdamped — decaying oscillation|

**Overdamped case** ($c^2 - 4mk > 0$, equivalently $c > 2\sqrt{mk}$):

$$r_2 < -\frac{c}{2m} < r_1 < 0$$

$$\boxed{y = c_1 e^{r_1 t} + c_2 e^{r_2 t}}$$

As $t \to \infty$: $y \to 0$ (both exponentials decay).

> [!example] Graph description (Slide 32) **Function plotted:** $y = c_1 e^{r_1 t} + c_2 e^{r_2 t}$ (overdamped)
> 
> - **Several curves** shown for different initial conditions — some start positive, some negative, some with a hump before decaying
> - All curves approach $y = 0$ asymptotically, never crossing zero more than once
> - No oscillation — the mass just creeps back to equilibrium
> - The slower decay rate $r_1$ (less negative) dominates for large $t$, so all curves eventually behave like $c_1 e^{r_1 t}$

---

### Slide 33–36 · Damped Example (Overdamped)

**Problem:** A 1 kg mass stretches a spring 50 cm at equilibrium and is suspended in a dashpot with damping constant $c = 10$. The mass is displaced **downward** 40 cm and released from rest.

**Step 1 — Units (MKS: m, kg, s)**

$$g = 9.8;\text{m/s}^2, \qquad \Delta L = 0.5;\text{m}$$

$$k = \frac{mg}{\Delta L} = \frac{1 \times 9.8}{0.5} = 19.6;\text{N/m}$$

**Step 2 — ODE**

$$y'' + 10y' + 19.6y = 0$$

**Step 3 — Initial conditions** (displaced downward = negative, released = zero velocity)

$$y(0) = -0.4;\text{m}, \qquad y'(0) = 0$$

**Step 4 — Characteristic equation**

$$r^2 + 10r + 19.6 = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad r = \frac{-10 \pm \sqrt{100 - 78.4}}{2} = \frac{-10 \pm \sqrt{21.6}}{2}$$

$$r_1 \approx -2.6762, \qquad r_2 \approx -7.3238$$

Check: $c^2 - 4mk = 100 - 78.4 = 21.6 > 0$ → **overdamped** ✓

**Step 5 — General solution**

$$y = c_1 e^{-2.6762t} + c_2 e^{-7.3238t}$$

**Step 6 — Apply initial conditions**

$$y(0) = c_1 + c_2 = -0.4$$

$$y'(0) = -2.6762,c_1 - 7.3238,c_2 = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad c_1 = -\frac{7.3238}{2.6762}c_2$$

Solving the system: $c_1 \approx -0.63$, $c_2 \approx 0.23$

**Solution:**

$$\boxed{y \approx -0.63,e^{-2.6762t} + 0.23,e^{-7.3238t}}$$

> [!example] Graph description (Slide 36) **Function plotted:** $y \approx -0.63e^{-2.6762t} + 0.23e^{-7.3238t}$
> 
> - Starts at $y = -0.4$ m (below equilibrium)
> - The $e^{-7.3238t}$ term decays very fast — gone by $t \approx 1$ s
> - The $e^{-2.6762t}$ term decays more slowly, dominating the long-run behavior
> - The curve rises smoothly toward zero, reaching approximately $y = 0$ by $t \approx 2$ s
> - **No oscillation** — purely exponential return to equilibrium (overdamped behavior)

## Summary — Three Models

|Model|ODE|Behavior|Solution form|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Undamped, unforced**|$my'' + ky = 0$|Perpetual oscillation|$c_1\cos(\omega_0 t) + c_2\sin(\omega_0 t)$|
|**Undamped, forced** ($\omega \neq \omega_0$)|$my'' + ky = F_0\cos(\omega t)$|Beats|$\frac{F_0}{m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)}(\cos\omega t - \cos\omega_0 t)$|
|**Undamped, resonance** ($\omega = \omega_0$)|$my'' + ky = F_0\cos(\omega_0 t)$|Unbounded growth|$\frac{F_0}{2m\omega_0}t\sin(\omega_0 t)$|
|**Overdamped** ($c^2 > 4mk$)|$my'' + cy' + ky = 0$|Exponential decay, no oscillation|$c_1 e^{r_1 t} + c_2 e^{r_2 t}$|

> [!tip] Applications of spring–mass models These equations model far more than mechanical springs: electrical LC and RLC circuits, acoustic resonance, structural vibrations in bridges and buildings, and even quantum mechanical oscillators all obey the same mathematical structure.

$$\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \qquad\text{(natural frequency)}$$

$$T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0} \qquad\text{(period)}$$

$$r = \frac{-c \pm \sqrt{c^2 - 4mk}}{2m} \qquad\text{(damped characteristic roots)}$$
