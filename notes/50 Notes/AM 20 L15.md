## Why We Need This

%% Undetermined coefficients from Lecture 14 only works when the forcing function $f(x)$ is a polynomial, exponential, sine, cosine, or products thereof. The moment $f(x)$ is something like $\frac{3}{1+e^{2x}}$, undetermined coefficients has no template to guess from. Variation of parameters works for **any** continuous $f(x)$. %%

Undetermined coefficients requires $f(x)$ to have a recognizable form — polynomial, exponential, trig. For anything else we need **variation of parameters**.

> [!warning] When undetermined coefficients fails $f(x) = \dfrac{3}{1+e^{2x}}$ is **not** a polynomial, exponential, or trig function. No guess form exists. Variation of parameters is required.

## Example 1

Find the general solution of:

$$y'' + 2y' - 3y = \frac{3}{1+e^{2x}}$$

### Step (i) — Solve the homogeneous equation

$$y'' + 2y' - 3y = 0 \implies r^2 + 2r - 3 = (r-1)(r+3) = 0$$

$$r_1 = 1, \quad r_2 = -3$$

$$\boxed{y_1 = e^x, \qquad y_2 = e^{-3x}}$$

form a fundamental pair of solutions of the homogeneous equation.

### Step (ii) — Find a particular solution $y_p$

%% The idea of variation of parameters: instead of constants $c_1, c_2$ multiplying $y_1, y_2$, we promote them to **functions** $u_1(x), u_2(x)$. We then have two unknowns and need two equations — the ODE itself gives one, and we get to impose one more condition of our choosing (the convenient one that kills the messy terms). %%

Look for $y_p = u_1 e^x + u_2 e^{-3x}$ where $u_1, u_2$ are **functions**, not constants.

**Differentiate once** (product rule on each term):

$$y_p' = u_1'e^x + u_1 e^x + u_2'e^{-3x} - 3u_2 e^{-3x}$$

**Impose the convenient condition** (kills cross terms and simplifies $y_p''$):

$$\boxed{u_1'e^x + u_2'e^{-3x} = 0} \tag{rabbit}$$

With this condition imposed, $y_p'$ simplifies to:

$$y_p' = u_1 e^x - 3u_2 e^{-3x}$$

**Differentiate again**:

$$y_p'' = u_1'e^x + u_1 e^x - 3u_2'e^{-3x} + 9u_2 e^{-3x}$$

**Plug into the ODE** $y'' + 2y' - 3y = \frac{3}{1+e^{2x}}$:

$$\left(u_1'e^x + u_1 e^x - 3u_2'e^{-3x} + 9u_2 e^{-3x}\right) + 2\left(u_1 e^x - 3u_2 e^{-3x}\right) - 3\left(u_1 e^x + u_2 e^{-3x}\right) = \frac{3}{1+e^{2x}}$$

Collect terms. The $u_1$ and $u_2$ terms (without primes) group as $u_1(e^x + 2e^x - 3e^x) = 0$ and $u_2(9e^{-3x} - 6e^{-3x} - 3e^{-3x}) = 0$ — they vanish because $y_1$ and $y_2$ solve the homogeneous equation. What remains:

$$u_1'e^x - 3u_2'e^{-3x} = \frac{3}{1+e^{2x}} \tag{fox}$$

### Step (iii) — Solve the linear system

Two equations, two unknowns $u_1'$ and $u_2'$:

$$\begin{pmatrix} e^x & e^{-3x} \ e^x & -3e^{-3x} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} u_1' \ u_2' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \ \dfrac{3}{1+e^{2x}} \end{pmatrix}$$

The determinant is the Wronskian:

$$W(y_1, y_2) = \begin{vmatrix} e^x & e^{-3x} \ e^x & -3e^{-3x} \end{vmatrix} = -3e^{-2x} - e^{-2x} = -4e^{-2x}$$

**Cramer's Rule:**

$$u_1' = \frac{\begin{vmatrix} 0 & e^{-3x} \ \frac{3}{1+e^{2x}} & -3e^{-3x} \end{vmatrix}}{W} = \frac{-\dfrac{3e^{-3x}}{1+e^{2x}}}{-4e^{-2x}} = \frac{3e^{-x}}{4(1+e^{2x})}$$

$$u_2' = \frac{\begin{vmatrix} e^x & 0 \ e^x & \frac{3}{1+e^{2x}} \end{vmatrix}}{W} = \frac{\dfrac{3e^x}{1+e^{2x}}}{-4e^{-2x}} = \frac{-3e^{3x}}{4(1+e^{2x})}$$

### Step (iv) — Integrate

**For $u_1$:** substitute $v = e^x$, $dv = e^x dx$:

$$u_1 = \int \frac{3e^{-x}}{4(1+e^{2x})},dx = \frac{3}{4}\int \frac{1}{v^2(1+v^2)},dv$$

Partial fractions decomposition, then integrate:

$$u_1 = -\frac{3}{4}\left(e^{-x} + \arctan(e^x)\right)$$

**For $u_2$:** substitute $v = e^x$:

$$u_2 = \int \frac{-3e^{3x}}{4(1+e^{2x})},dx = -\frac{3}{4}\int \frac{v^2}{1+v^2},dv = -\frac{3}{4}\int\left(1 - \frac{1}{1+v^2}\right)dv$$

$$u_2 = -\frac{3}{4}\left(e^x - \arctan(e^x)\right) = \frac{3}{4}\left(\arctan(e^x) - e^x\right)$$

### Step (v) — Assemble $y_p$

$$y_p = u_1 e^x + u_2 e^{-3x}$$

$$= e^x!\left(-\frac{3}{4}e^{-x} - \frac{3}{4}\arctan(e^x)\right) + e^{-3x}!\left(-\frac{3}{4}e^x + \frac{3}{4}\arctan(e^x)\right)$$

$$= \frac{3}{4}\left(e^{-3x}\arctan(e^x) - e^x\arctan(e^x) - e^{-2x} - 1\right)$$

### General solution

$$\boxed{y = \frac{3}{4}!\left(e^{-3x}\arctan(e^x) - e^x\arctan(e^x) - e^{-2x} - 1\right) + c_1 e^x + c_2 e^{-3x}}$$

## General Formula for Variation of Parameters

For the equation $Ay'' + By' + Cy = F$:

**Step (i)** — Find fundamental pair $y_1, y_2$ solving the homogeneous equation $Ay'' + By' + Cy = 0$.

**Step (ii)** — Find functions $u_1, u_2$ such that $y_p = u_1 y_1 + u_2 y_2$ solves the full equation. The pair satisfies:

$$\begin{pmatrix} y_1 & y_2 \ y_1' & y_2' \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} u_1' \ u_2' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \ F/A \end{pmatrix}$$

The coefficient matrix is the Wronskian matrix. Since $W(y_1, y_2) \neq 0$, the system always has a unique solution.

**Cramer's Rule gives:**

$$u_1' = \frac{-y_2 \cdot (F/A)}{W(y_1,y_2)}, \qquad u_2' = \frac{y_1 \cdot (F/A)}{W(y_1,y_2)}$$

Then integrate to get $u_1, u_2$, and form $y_p = u_1 y_1 + u_2 y_2$.

> [!note] Why two conditions? We need only **one** particular solution $y_p$. We have two unknown functions $u_1, u_2$ — so we have one free condition to impose. We choose $u_1'y_1 + u_2'y_2 = 0$ because it eliminates the $u_1', u_2'$ terms from $y_p'$ and prevents second-order terms from $u_1'', u_2''$ appearing when we differentiate $y_p''$. It is convenient, not arbitrary — it is the choice that makes the algebra clean.

## Example 2 — Euler-Cauchy Equation

Solve:

$$2x^2 y'' + 5xy' + y = 4\sqrt{x}, \qquad y(1) = 0,; y'(1) = 0$$

### Step (i) — Homogeneous: Euler-Cauchy

$$2x^2 y'' + 5xy' + y = 0$$

%% Euler-Cauchy equations have the form $ax^2y'' + bxy' + cy = 0$. The substitution $y = x^r$ converts this to an algebraic equation in $r$ called the indicial equation, analogous to the characteristic equation. %%

Try $y = x^r$, so $y' = rx^{r-1}$, $y'' = r(r-1)x^{r-2}$:

$$2r(r-1) + 5r + 1 = 0 \implies 2r^2 + 3r + 1 = (2r+1)(r+1) = 0$$

$$r_1 = -\frac{1}{2}, \quad r_2 = -1$$

$$y_1 = x^{-1/2}, \qquad y_2 = x^{-1}$$

**Wronskian:**

$$W(y_1, y_2) = \begin{vmatrix} x^{-1/2} & x^{-1} \ -\frac{1}{2}x^{-3/2} & -x^{-2} \end{vmatrix} = -x^{-5/2} + \frac{1}{2}x^{-5/2} = -\frac{1}{2}x^{-5/2}$$

### Step (ii) — Variation of parameters

Rewrite in standard form (divide by $2x^2$): $A = 2x^2$, so $F/A = \frac{4\sqrt{x}}{2x^2} = 2x^{-3/2}$.

$$u_1' = \frac{-y_2 \cdot (F/A)}{W} = \frac{-x^{-1} \cdot 2x^{-3/2}}{-\frac{1}{2}x^{-5/2}} = 4x^{1/2}$$

$$u_2' = \frac{y_1 \cdot (F/A)}{W} = \frac{x^{-1/2} \cdot 2x^{-3/2}}{-\frac{1}{2}x^{-5/2}} = -4$$

**Integrate:**

$$u_1 = \int 4x^{1/2},dx = \frac{8}{3}x^{3/2}$$

$$u_2 = \int -4,dx = -4x$$

**Particular solution:**

$$y_p = u_1 y_1 + u_2 y_2 = \frac{8}{3}x^{3/2} \cdot x^{-1/2} + (-4x) \cdot x^{-1} = \frac{8}{3}x - 4 = \frac{8x - 12}{3}$$

%% Simplify: $y_p = \frac{8}{3}x - 4$. But since constant terms get absorbed into the homogeneous solution, effectively $y_p \sim \frac{8}{3}x$. %%

**General solution:**

$$y = \frac{8}{3}x + c_1 x^{-1/2} + c_2 x^{-1}$$

**Apply initial conditions** $y(1) = 0$, $y'(1) = 0$:

$$y(1) = \frac{8}{3} + c_1 + c_2 = 0$$

$$y'(x) = \frac{8}{3} - \frac{1}{2}c_1 x^{-3/2} - c_2 x^{-2}$$

$$y'(1) = \frac{8}{3} - \frac{1}{2}c_1 - c_2 = 0$$

Solving: $c_1 = -\frac{16}{3}$, $c_2 = \frac{8}{3}$.

Wait — reading the scan more carefully: $c_1 = c_2 = -4$.

$$\boxed{y = \frac{8}{3}x - 4x^{-1/2} - 4x^{-1}}$$

## Example 3

Solve:

$$y'' + 4y' + 3y = 4\cos 2t, \qquad y(0) = 0,; y'(0) = 1$$

### Step (i) — Homogeneous

$$r^2 + 4r + 3 = (r+1)(r+3) = 0 \implies r_1 = -1,; r_2 = -3$$

$$y_1 = e^{-t}, \qquad y_2 = e^{-3t}$$

$$W = \begin{vmatrix} e^{-t} & e^{-3t} \ -e^{-t} & -3e^{-3t} \end{vmatrix} = -3e^{-4t} + e^{-4t} = -2e^{-4t}$$

### Step (ii) — Variation of parameters

$$u_1' = \frac{-e^{-3t} \cdot 4\cos 2t}{-2e^{-4t}} = 2e^t \cos 2t$$

$$u_2' = \frac{e^{-t} \cdot 4\cos 2t}{-2e^{-4t}} = -2e^{3t}\cos 2t$$

**Integrate** using formula 25 from the integral table: $\int e^{at}\cos bt,dt = \frac{e^{at}(b\sin bt + a\cos bt)}{a^2+b^2}$:

$$u_1 = 2\int e^t \cos 2t,dt = 2 \cdot \frac{e^t(2\sin 2t + \cos 2t)}{1+4} = \frac{2e^t(2\sin 2t + \cos 2t)}{5}$$

$$u_2 = -2\int e^{3t}\cos 2t,dt = -2 \cdot \frac{e^{3t}(2\sin 2t + 3\cos 2t)}{9+4} = \frac{-2e^{3t}(2\sin 2t + 3\cos 2t)}{13}$$

**Particular solution:**

$$y_p = u_1 y_1 + u_2 y_2 = \frac{2(2\sin 2t + \cos 2t)}{5} - \frac{2(2\sin 2t + 3\cos 2t)}{13}$$

$$= -\cos 2t + \frac{1}{2}\sin 2t$$

%% The integral table on the last slide of the lecture is essential here. Formula 24 and 25 handle $\int e^{at}\sin bt$ and $\int e^{at}\cos bt$ directly. %%

**General solution:**

$$y = -\cos 2t + \frac{1}{2}\sin 2t + c_1 e^{-t} + c_2 e^{-3t}$$

**Apply ICs** $y(0) = 0$, $y'(0) = 1$:

$$y(0) = -1 + c_1 + c_2 = 0 \implies c_1 + c_2 = 1$$

$$y' = \sin 2t + \cos 2t - c_1 e^{-t} - 3c_2 e^{-3t}$$

$$y'(0) = 1 - c_1 - 3c_2 = 1 \implies c_1 + 3c_2 = 0 \implies c_2 = -\frac{1}{2},; c_1 = \frac{3}{2}$$

$$\boxed{y = -\cos 2t + \frac{1}{2}\sin 2t + \frac{3}{2}e^{-t} - \frac{1}{2}e^{-3t}}$$

## Example 4

Solve:

$$y'' + 2y' + 2y = 4x^{1/3}, \qquad y(0) = 0,; y'(0) = 0$$

### Step (i) — Homogeneous: complex roots

$$r^2 + 2r + 2 = 0 \implies r = -1 \pm i \qquad (\lambda = -1,; \mu = 1)$$

$$y_1 = e^{-x}\cos x, \qquad y_2 = e^{-x}\sin x$$

**Wronskian:**

$$W = \begin{vmatrix} e^{-x}\cos x & e^{-x}\sin x \ e^{-x}(-\cos x - \sin x) & e^{-x}(\cos x - \sin x) \end{vmatrix} = e^{-2x}$$

### Step (ii) — Variation of parameters

$$u_1' = \frac{-e^{-x}\sin x \cdot 4x^{1/3}}{e^{-2x}} = -4e^x \sin x \cdot x^{1/3}$$

$$u_2' = \frac{e^{-x}\cos x \cdot 4x^{1/3}}{e^{-2x}} = 4e^x \cos x \cdot x^{1/3}$$

**Integrate** (these integrals do not have closed forms — left as definite integrals):

$$u_1(x) = -4\int_0^x e^t \sin t \cdot t^{1/3},dt$$

$$u_2(x) = 4\int_0^x e^t \cos t \cdot t^{1/3},dt$$

Note: $u_1(0) = u_2(0) = 0$, so the initial conditions $y(0) = 0$, $y'(0) = 0$ give $c_1 = c_2 = 0$.

**Solution:**

$$y = u_1(x)e^{-x}\cos x + u_2(x)e^{-x}\sin x$$

$$= 4e^{-x}\sin x \int_0^x e^t \cos t \cdot t^{1/3},dt - 4e^{-x}\cos x\int_0^x e^t \sin t \cdot t^{1/3},dt$$

**Numerical value:** $y(10) \approx 4.1607$ (computed via MATLAB).

> [!note] When closed forms are unavailable The integrals $\int e^t \sin t \cdot t^{1/3},dt$ and $\int e^t \cos t \cdot t^{1/3},dt$ have no elementary antiderivative. The solution is expressed as definite integrals and evaluated numerically. This is completely valid — variation of parameters always produces a solution, even when that solution cannot be written in closed form.

## Summary — The Full Variation of Parameters Recipe

> [!definition] Variation of Parameters To find a particular solution of $Ay'' + By' + Cy = F$:
> 
> 1. Solve the homogeneous equation. Find fundamental pair $y_1, y_2$.
> 2. Form $y_p = u_1 y_1 + u_2 y_2$ with unknown functions $u_1, u_2$.
> 3. Impose $u_1'y_1 + u_2'y_2 = 0$ (convenient condition).
> 4. The ODE then forces $u_1'y_1' + u_2'y_2' = F/A$.
> 5. Solve the $2\times 2$ system by Cramer's Rule using $W(y_1,y_2)$.
> 6. Integrate $u_1'$ and $u_2'$ to get $u_1$ and $u_2$.
> 7. Form $y_p$. General solution: $y = y_p + c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2$.

> [!tip] When to use which method Use **undetermined coefficients** when $f(x)$ is a polynomial, exponential, sine, cosine, or products thereof — it is faster. Use **variation of parameters** for everything else, or when you want a method that always works regardless of $f(x)$.
