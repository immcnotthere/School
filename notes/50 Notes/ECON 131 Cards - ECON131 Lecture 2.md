## Spot vs Forward Contract
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is a spot transaction? | An immediate exchange of two currencies at today's rate. | p.1; definition |
| What is a forward contract? | An agreement now on an exchange rate for a trade on a future date. | p.1; definition |
| What is a currency swap? | Exchanging currencies now and agreeing to reverse the exchange at a later date. | p.1; definition |
| What is a currency option? | The right, but not the obligation, to exchange at a pre-agreed rate on a set date. | p.1; definition |
| What is a derivative security? | An investment whose payoff is derived from the performance of an underlying asset (forwards, swaps and options are all derivatives). | p.1; definition |

## Direct vs Indirect Quote

| What is an indirect quote, from the US point of view? | Foreign currency per unit of domestic, e.g. ¥100/\$. | p.1; definition |

## Appreciation and Depreciation
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What does it mean for a currency to appreciate? | Its value rises in terms of another currency. | p.2; definition |
| Devalue vs depreciate: what's the difference? | Devaluation is a change made by the domestic government; depreciation is the market moving the value down. | p.2; discrimination |
| Formula for the rate of change of an exchange rate? | $\dfrac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}}$, and it describes the currency in the denominator. | p.2; formula |
| The pound's price goes from 1.50 to 1.80 dollars per pound. By what percent did the pound appreciate? | 20%: (1.80 − 1.50) / 1.50 = 0.20. | p.2; worked |
| ¥/\$ moves from 120 to 100. Which currency appreciated? | The yen: it now takes fewer yen to buy a dollar (the dollar depreciated). | class 9/29; transcript |
| \$/£ rises from 2.00 to 2.50. By what percent did the pound appreciate? | 25%: (2.50 − 2.00) / 2.00. | class 9/29; transcript |

## Devaluation and Trade
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A country devalues its currency. What happens to the price of its exports abroad? | They get cheaper for foreign buyers. | class 9/29; transcript |
| A country devalues its currency. What happens to the price of its imports at home? | They get more expensive. | class 9/29; transcript |
| A 100-yuan shirt: its dollar price at 4 yuan/\$, then after devaluation to 5 yuan/\$? | \$25, then \$20 (divide yuan by yuan per dollar). | class 9/29; transcript |
| A \$200 US computer: its yuan price at 4 yuan/\$, then at 5 yuan/\$? | 800 yuan, then 1,000 yuan (multiply dollars by yuan per dollar). | class 9/29; transcript |
| Who gains and who loses from a devaluation? | Exporters gain; households and importers lose because imports cost more. | class 9/29; transcript |
| Why has China managed its currency's value? | To keep the yuan low so its exports stay competitive (an export-led growth strategy). | class 9/29; transcript |

## Vehicle Currency and Cross Rates
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is a vehicle currency? | A currency used in many international transactions worldwide; the US dollar is on one side of about 89% of trades. | p.2; definition |
| What is a cross rate? | An exchange rate between two currencies, neither of which is the US dollar, e.g. £/€. | p.2; definition |
| How do you get the implied cross rate €/£ from €/\$ and \$/£? | Multiply: €/£ = (€/\$) × (\$/£); the dollars cancel. | p.2; procedure |

## Triangular Arbitrage
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is triangular arbitrage? | Trading through three currencies to profit when a quoted cross rate differs from the one implied through the dollar. | p.2; definition |
| What condition makes triangular arbitrage profitable? | Quoted €/£ ≠ (€/\$) × (\$/£). | p.2; key move |
| What does triangular arbitrage do to cross rates over time? | Keeps them in line with the dollar rates, because traders exploit any gap until it closes. | p.2; key move |
| Quoted €/£ is less than (€/\$) × (\$/£). Which way do you go around the triangle, starting from euros? | € → £ → \$ → €: buy the pound, which is cheap in the direct quote. | class 9/29; transcript |
| Quoted €/£ is greater than (€/\$) × (\$/£). Which way do you go, starting from euros? | € → \$ → £ → €: the other way around. | class 9/29; transcript |
| Does the arbitrage return depend on which currency, or how much, you start with? | No: the return is a ratio, the same whether you start with €10 or \$10,000. | class 9/29; transcript |

## Bid-Ask Spread
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is the bid rate? | The rate at which the bank buys the base currency. | p.2; definition |
| What is the ask rate? | The rate at which the bank sells the base currency. | p.2; definition |
| Formula for the bid-ask spread in percent? | $100 \times \dfrac{\text{ask} - \text{bid}}{\text{midpoint}}$ | p.2; formula |
| What is a pip? | The fourth decimal place of a currency quote. | p.2; definition |
| Typical bid-ask spread for major currencies? | About 0.05% to 0.07%, and lower for very liquid ones (about 0.03% for \$/€). | p.2; definition |
| Rule for who gets the worse rate in a bid-ask quote? | You always trade at the rate that is worse for you: the bank buys low (bid) and sells high (ask). | p.2; key move |

## Real Effective Exchange Rate (REER)
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is the real effective exchange rate (REER)? | A trade-weighted average of a currency's value against a basket of other currencies. | p.2; definition |
| How do you compute the change in REER? | Weight each bilateral change by that partner's share of trade, then add. | p.2; procedure |

## Exchange Rate Regimes (Peg and Managed Float)
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What was China's exchange rate regime for about 10 years, and what replaced it? | A fixed peg near 8.28 yuan per dollar, replaced by a managed float. | class 9/29; transcript |
| What is a managed float? | The rate mostly floats, but the central bank intervenes if it strays too far from its target. | class 9/29; transcript |

# Frankenstein problems

Do these on paper first, then check.

**Problem 1 (Lecture 2, triangular arbitrage).** Quotes: €1.2/£, €0.8/\$, \$2/£. Start with €10. Step 1: sell euros, buy pounds. Step 2: sell pounds, buy dollars. Step 3: sell dollars, buy euros. (a) How much after each step? (b) Rate of return?
Answer: implied €/£ = 0.8 × 2 = 1.6, but the quote is 1.2, so pounds are cheap in euros. Step 1: €10 / 1.2 = £8.33. Step 2: £8.33 × 2 = \$16.67. Step 3: \$16.67 × 0.8 = €13.33. Return: (13.33 − 10)/10 = 33.3%.

**Problem 2 (Lecture 2, bid-ask).** Mississippi Mud Pies needs CHF 150. The bank quotes CHF 1.2–1.5/USD, with USD as the base currency. Dollar cost?
Answer: the company sells dollars to the bank, and the bank buys the base currency (USD) at the bid, 1.2 CHF per dollar (the worse rate for the company). Cost = 150 / 1.2 = \$125.

**Problem 3 (Lecture 2, REER).** The US is 60% of UK trade and Japan 40%. The pound rises 20% against the dollar and falls 30% against the yen. REER change?
Answer: 0.6 × (+20%) + 0.4 × (−30%) = 12% − 12% = 0%. No change.

**Problem 4 (appreciation).** ¥/\$ goes from 100 to 110. Which currency appreciated, and by how much?
Answer: the quote prices the dollar (the denominator). (110 − 100)/100 = +10%, so the dollar appreciated 10%. The yen depreciated: \$/¥ went from 0.0100 to 0.00909, a change of −9.1%.
