# Population Parameters in Econometrics

> [!info] Core idea
> A **population parameter** is a fixed, usually unknown numerical characteristic of an entire population or data-generating process.

## 1. What is a population?

In econometrics, the **population** is the full set of units, outcomes, or possible observations we want to study.

Examples:
- all households in California
- all firms in an industry
- all possible wage outcomes generated by a labor market process
- all time periods produced by some stochastic process

A sample gives us data, but the **parameter** belongs to the population.

## 2. What is a population parameter?

A population parameter is a quantity that describes the population.

Common examples:
- population mean: $\mu = E[Y]$
- population variance: $\sigma^2 = \mathrm{Var}(Y)$
- population covariance: $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)$
- population correlation: $\rho_{XY}$
- population regression coefficients: $\beta_0, \beta_1, \dots, \beta_k$

These are:
- **fixed**
- usually **unknown**
- targets of estimation and inference

> [!important]
> Parameters do **not** vary across samples.  
> **Estimators** and **estimates** do.

## 3. Parameter vs estimator vs estimate

| Concept | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Parameter | True population quantity | $\beta_1$ |
| Estimator | Rule for computing from data | $\hat{\beta}_1$ as OLS formula |
| Estimate | Realized numerical value from one sample | $\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.84$ |

A good way to think about it:

- **parameter** = truth in the population
- **estimator** = recipe
- **estimate** = result from your sample

## 4. Population parameters in the linear model

A standard population linear model is:

$$
Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \cdots + \beta_k X_k + u
$$

Here:

- $Y$ = dependent variable
- $X_1, \dots, X_k$ = regressors
- $u$ = error term
- $\beta_0, \beta_1, \dots, \beta_k$ = **population parameters**

These coefficients summarize the population relationship between $Y$ and the regressors.

### Interpretation of $\beta_j$

In many econometrics settings, $\beta_j$ is interpreted as:

> the change in the population conditional mean of $Y$ associated with a one-unit change in $X_j$, holding other regressors fixed

Formally,

$$
E[Y \mid X_1,\dots,X_k] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \cdots + \beta_k X_k
$$

when the conditional expectation function is linear.

## 5. Population regression function

The **population regression function (PRF)** is:

$$
E[Y \mid X] 
$$

In a simple linear model:

$$
E[Y \mid X] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X
$$

This is a population object.  
It describes the true average relationship in the population.

The sample analogue is the **sample regression function**:

$$
\hat{Y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 X
$$

## 6. Why parameters matter

Econometrics is usually about learning unknown population quantities from sample data.

Typical goals:
- estimate the average effect of education on wages
- estimate the elasticity of demand
- estimate the return to capital
- test whether a population coefficient equals zero
- construct confidence intervals for a parameter

So the workflow is:

1. define the population parameter of interest
2. choose an estimator
3. use sample data to compute an estimate
4. evaluate uncertainty with standard errors, tests, and confidence intervals

## 7. Examples of important population parameters

### A. Population mean

If $Y$ is wage:

$$
\mu = E[Y]
$$

This is the average wage in the population.

Sample analogue:

$$
\bar{Y} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i
$$

### B. Population variance

$$
\sigma^2 = \mathrm{Var}(Y)
$$

Measures dispersion in the population.

### C. Population proportion

If $D$ is an indicator variable equal to 1 if employed:

$$
p = E[D]
$$

Because for an indicator variable, the mean equals the proportion.

### D. Population covariance

$$
\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = E[(X-E[X])(Y-E[Y])]
$$

Shows whether two variables move together in the population.

### E. Population regression slope

In simple regression:

$$
\beta_1 = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)}{\mathrm{Var}(X)}
$$

under the linear projection interpretation.

This is often one of the main parameters of interest.

## 8. Structural parameters vs descriptive parameters

> [!note]
> Not every parameter has the same interpretation.

### Descriptive parameter
Summarizes association or distribution.

Examples:
- mean income
- variance of test scores
- regression slope as best linear predictor

### Structural parameter
Intended to capture a deeper economic mechanism, often causal.

Examples:
- causal effect of schooling on earnings
- price elasticity from a structural demand model
- treatment effect of a policy intervention

In applied econometrics, a major challenge is determining whether an estimated coefficient is merely descriptive or can be interpreted causally.

## 9. Finite population vs superpopulation

There are two common ways to think about population parameters.

### Finite population
The population is a fixed set of units, like all 10,000 students in a district.

Parameter example:

$$
\mu = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} Y_i
$$

### Superpopulation
The observed data are viewed as draws from a broader stochastic process.

Parameter example:

$$
\mu = E[Y]
$$

Econometrics often uses the **superpopulation** view, especially in probability-based inference.

## 10. Parameters in causal econometrics

In causal inference, population parameters are often defined as treatment effects.

Examples:

### Average treatment effect (ATE)

$$
ATE = E[Y(1) - Y(0)]
$$

### Average treatment effect on the treated (ATT)

$$
ATT = E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid D=1]
$$

These are population parameters because they refer to averages over the full target population.

## 11. Identification and estimation

A key distinction:

### Parameter
What you want to know.

### Identification
Whether the parameter can, in principle, be learned from the population distribution of observed data.

### Estimation
How you approximate it using a sample.

So there are three separate questions:

1. **What is the parameter?**
2. **Is it identified?**
3. **How do we estimate it?**

> [!warning]
> A parameter can exist but still be **unidentified** from the available data.

## 12. Example: wage regression

Suppose the population model is

$$
wage = \beta_0 + \beta_1 education + u
$$

Then:

- $\beta_0$ = intercept in the population
- $\beta_1$ = population slope on education
- $E[u \mid education] = 0$ is an assumption that helps identify $\beta_1$

If $\beta_1 = 0.08$, one interpretation is:

> one additional year of education is associated with an 8% increase in wages

That interpretation depends on the exact model form, such as whether wage is in logs.

## 13. Common confusion points

### Confusion 1: "The parameter changes with the sample"
False.  
The **estimate** changes with the sample. The **parameter** is fixed.

### Confusion 2: "A statistically significant estimate proves the parameter is large"
False.  
Significance depends on both effect size and uncertainty.

### Confusion 3: "Regression coefficients are always causal parameters"
False.  
They may only describe conditional association unless identification assumptions justify a causal interpretation.

## 14. Quick intuition

Think of econometrics as trying to see a hidden object through noisy data.

- the **population parameter** is the hidden object
- the **sample** is your limited view
- the **estimator** is the lens
- the **estimate** is what you currently see

## 15. Summary

> [!summary]
> Population parameters are fixed but unknown quantities that describe the full population or data-generating process.
>
> In econometrics, they are the main objects of interest:
> - means
> - variances
> - covariances
> - correlations
> - regression coefficients
> - treatment effects
>
> Econometric analysis uses sample data to estimate these parameters and make inferences about them.
