# ECON 131 Cards - ECON131 Dollar Dominance USD Strength and Structural Dominance

## Trade-Weighted Dollar Index
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What does the trade-weighted (broad) dollar index measure? | The US dollar's value against a weighted basket of foreign currencies, based on trade importance. | p.2; definition |
| When did the broad dollar index peak in the 2022 to 2023 episode, and how strong was it historically? | Late 2022; it was the strongest the dollar had been since 1985. | p.2; definition |

## Currency Appreciation and Quote Direction
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How is the percent appreciation of the USD against a currency calculated from exchange rate quotes $E_0$ and $E_1$ (units of foreign currency per USD)? | $\dfrac{E_1 - E_0}{E_0}$ | p.2; formula |
| In 2022 the dollar appreciated 11.5% against the Japanese yen. Did it take more or fewer yen to buy one dollar after this move? | More yen: a dollar appreciation against a currency means one dollar now buys more units of that currency. | p.2; definition |
| Why is a currency's X% appreciation against currency B not the same size as a X% depreciation of B against it? | Percent changes are not symmetric under reciprocals: a move from $E_0$ to $E_1$ is a $(E_1-E_0)/E_0$ gain measured one way, but only a $(E_1-E_0)/E_1$ loss measured the other way, and those two percentages differ in size. | p.2; discrimination |

## Interest Rate Differential
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is an interest rate differential in this context? | The gap between one country's central bank policy rate and another's, e.g. the Fed's rate minus the ECB's rate. | p.3; definition |
| Why does a wider US interest rate differential (Fed rate above other central banks) strengthen the dollar? | Higher US rates make dollar assets more attractive, pulling global capital into the US and raising demand for dollars. | p.3; definition |

## Fed Policy Pivot and Dollar Appreciation Mechanism
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trace the 2022 mechanism from US inflation to dollar strength. | 9.1% US inflation forced aggressive Fed rate hikes (75 bp increments); since the ECB and BoJ hiked much more slowly, the US interest rate differential widened; this pulled capital inflows into the US, raising demand for and the value of the dollar. | p.3; procedure |
| In the 2022 to 2023 episode, which central bank moved its policy rate the least, and what does that imply for its currency versus the dollar? | The Bank of Japan (BoJ), staying near zero; this created the widest interest rate gap versus the Fed, implying the strongest pressure for yen depreciation against the dollar. | p.3; discrimination |

## Flight to Safety (Safe Haven Demand)
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is a flight to safety? | A shift of global investor capital into perceived safe, liquid assets, such as US Treasuries, during times of crisis or uncertainty. | p.4; definition |
| Name two 2022 shocks that triggered flight-to-safety dollar demand. | Russia's invasion of Ukraine (and the resulting European energy crisis), and China's growth slowdown from strict COVID lockdowns. | p.4; definition |

## Strong Dollar Spillovers (Winners and Losers)
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How does a strong dollar affect US consumers? | Imports become cheaper, which helps dampen domestic US inflation. | p.5; definition |
| How does a strong dollar affect US exporters? | American products become more expensive for foreign buyers, reducing US industries' global competitiveness. | p.5; definition |
| How does a strong dollar affect emerging market economies? | Their USD-denominated debt becomes costlier to service and repay, and capital outflows tighten local financial conditions. | p.5; definition |

## Exporting Inflation Channel (USD Commodity Pricing)
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stage 1 of the exporting inflation channel: how are major global commodities priced? | Oil, grains, and most industrial metals are priced globally in US dollars. | p.6; definition |
| Stage 2 (the double whammy): what happens to non-US countries when the dollar strengthens? | They face high dollar commodity prices and a weakening local currency at the same time, so it takes much more local currency to buy the same essentials. | p.6; procedure |
| Stage 3: how are foreign central banks forced to respond, and why is this a problem? | They are forced to hike interest rates to prevent currency collapse, even if their own economy is weak and cannot afford tighter policy. | p.6; procedure |

## Dollar's Outsized Financial Footprint
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is the US share of global GDP, versus the dollar's share of global FX transactions? | The US is about 26% of global GDP, but the dollar is on one side of about 88% of global foreign exchange transactions. | p.8; definition |
| What share of world debt and world trade invoicing use the US dollar? | About 64% of world debt is denominated in USD, and about 54% of global trade is invoiced in USD. | p.8; definition |

## Safe Haven Moat (Pillars of Dollar Dominance)
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What are the two structural pillars supporting the dollar's dominance? | Unmatched market depth (the deep, liquid US Treasury market as a source of safe assets) and institutional quality (strong legal framework, property rights, and an independent central bank). | p.9; definition |

## Dollar Smile Theory
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Describe the Dollar Smile Theory's two conditions under which the USD strengthens. | It strengthens during global turmoil (flight to safety) and during US economic exceptionalism/strong US growth (attracting investment for high yields). | p.9; definition |
| According to the Dollar Smile Theory, when is the dollar weakest? | In the middle of the curve, during periods of merely sluggish (neither crisis nor booming) global growth. | p.9; discrimination |

## Lack of Credible Dollar Alternatives
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Why does the euro fail to fully rival the dollar as a reserve currency? | It lacks a single, unified eurozone sovereign bond market; credit risk is fragmented across individual member states. | p.10; definition |
| Why is the Chinese renminbi's global role limited despite China's economic size? | China's capital controls and lack of financial openness constrain it; the renminbi holds only about 2.4% of global reserves. | p.10; definition |
| Why can't gold or currencies like the Australian or Canadian dollar replace the USD as the global anchor? | They lack the sheer scale and liquidity needed to anchor the global financial system, even as central banks diversify into them. | p.10; discrimination |

## Exorbitant Privilege
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is exorbitant privilege? | The set of unique benefits the US gets from issuing the world's dominant reserve currency, especially lower borrowing costs and seigniorage. | p.11; definition |
| How much higher can the US sustain its debt burden (as % of GDP) because of this privilege, per the source? | About 22 percentage points of GDP higher than it otherwise could. | p.11; definition |
| What is seigniorage in this context? | The benefit the US gets because foreigners hold trillions in US banknotes and dollar assets, which effectively function as interest-free loans to the US. | p.11; definition |

## Dutch Disease Argument (Cost of Exorbitant Privilege)
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is the Dutch Disease argument applied to the dollar? | Persistent global demand for dollars, from its reserve role, keeps the dollar structurally overvalued, which hurts the competitiveness of US manufacturing and worsens trade deficits. | p.11; discrimination |

## 2026 Dollar Outlook (Cyclical versus Structural Risks)
| Front | Back | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is the near-term (2026+) base case for the dollar, and why? | A gradual cyclical weakening, as the Federal Reserve begins cutting interest rates, narrowing the interest rate differential that had supported the dollar. | p.12; definition |
| What are the two emerging long-term risks to the dollar's dominance, per the source? | Uncertainty about the long-term independence of the Federal Reserve, and the sustainability of rising US fiscal deficits. | p.12; definition |

# Frankenstein problems

Numbers in these problems are illustrative (made up for practice), not figures from the lecture.

**Problem 1**
In 2022 the USD/JPY quote moved from ¥120 per dollar to ¥133.80 per dollar.

(a) Using the class formula, verify this is an 11.5% dollar appreciation against the yen.
(b) Using the Fed policy pivot mechanism, explain why the dollar rose more against the yen than it likely did against the euro that year.
(c) Which pillar of the dollar's safe haven moat is most directly responsible for capital continuing to flow into US assets during this period despite a simultaneous European energy crisis?

**Solution**
(a) $(E_1-E_0)/E_0 = (133.80-120)/120 = 13.80/120 = 0.115 = 11.5\%$. Confirmed.
(b) The Bank of Japan held its policy rate near zero throughout 2022 to 2023 while the Fed hiked aggressively (75 bp moves) to fight 9.1% inflation, so the US-Japan interest rate differential widened the most of any pair; the ECB, by contrast, also raised rates (just more slowly than the Fed), so the US-euro differential widened less. A wider differential pulls in more capital and produces more dollar appreciation, so JPY depreciated against the dollar by more than EUR did.
(c) Market depth: the deep, liquid US Treasury market gives global investors fleeing the European energy crisis and general turmoil somewhere large and safe to put capital, which is what let the flight-to-safety demand keep flowing into US assets.

Concepts tested: Currency Appreciation and Quote Direction; Fed Policy Pivot and Dollar Appreciation Mechanism; Safe Haven Moat (Pillars of Dollar Dominance).

**Problem 2**
Emerging-market country Z imports all of its oil (priced in USD) and carries $50 billion of USD-denominated government debt. This year the dollar strengthens 15% against Z's local currency.

(a) Using the exporting inflation channel, describe the "double whammy" country Z now faces on its oil purchases.
(b) Approximately how much more local currency does country Z need to raise to service the same $50 billion of USD debt, in percentage terms?
(c) Give one reason, tied to the dollar's outsized financial footprint or exorbitant privilege, that global capital keeps flowing into US assets even while this is straining country Z.

**Solution**
(a) Oil is priced in USD (Stage 1), so when the dollar strengthens 15% against Z's currency, Z faces both a high dollar oil price and a currency that buys 15% less of that dollar price than before, i.e. the combination of costly oil and a weak local currency at once (Stage 2's double whammy).
(b) Since the debt is fixed at $50 billion, and the dollar is now worth about 15% more in local currency terms, the local-currency cost of servicing that debt rises by roughly 15% as well.
(c) About 64% of world debt is denominated in USD and the dollar is on one side of about 88% of global FX transactions, so structural global demand for dollars (part of the exorbitant privilege the reserve-currency role grants the US) remains high regardless of the strain it places on individual emerging markets like Z.

Concepts tested: Exporting Inflation Channel (USD Commodity Pricing); Strong Dollar Spillovers (Winners and Losers); Dollar's Outsized Financial Footprint.
