## Slide 2 — The Role of Factor Endowments: Model Comparison

### [[Ricardian Model]]
- Trade driven by **differences in labor productivity** across industries and countries
- Differences in [[factor endowments]] are **ignored**
- Only one factor ([[Labor]]) → [[distributional effects of trade]] are ignored

### [[Specific Factors Model]]
- [[Comparative Advantage]] arises from cross-country differences in **factor endowments** and **sector factor specificity**
- Assumes **limited mobility** of [[Labor]] → well suited for examining distributional effects in the **short run**

## Slide 3 & 5 — The Role of Factor Endowments: H-O Model

### [[Heckscher-Ohlin Model]]

- Focus on cross-country differences in endowments of:
  - Skilled and unskilled [[Labor]]
  - Physical [[Capital]]
  - [[Land]]
  - Other factors of production

- [[Comparative Advantage]] emerges from the **interaction** of:
  1. **[[Relative Factor Abundance]]** — how much of each factor a country has
  2. **[[Relative Factor Intensity]]** — how intensively each factor is used in each sector

- **All factors are perfectly mobile** across sectors

> ☞ Factor mobility allows studying the **long-run consequences** of trade (cf. [[Specific Factors Model]] = short run)

## Slide 9 — H-O Model Assumptions (Part 1)

### Setup

Two countries: **Home** and **Foreign (\*)** | Two goods: **Food (F)** and **Clothing (C)** | Two factors: **[[Land]] (T)** and **[[Labor]] (L)**

### Key Assumptions

**⭐ Key #1 — [[Relative Factor Intensity]]:**
> The technology for producing **Food is more land-intensive** than the technology for producing Clothing

**⭐ Key #2 — Full Factor Mobility:**
> **Both** factors of production ([[Land]] and [[Labor]]) are **mobile** across sectors

(This distinguishes H-O from the [[Specific Factors Model]], where only [[Labor]] is mobile)

**Other Assumptions:**
- Constant supply of [[Land]] and [[Labor]] in each country; countries may differ in endowments
- [[Constant Returns to Scale]] (CRS) in production of both goods
- [[Diminishing Marginal Returns]] to each input
- [[Factor Complementarity]] of inputs

### 📝 Notes
>

## Slide 10 — H-O Model Assumptions (Part 2)

### [[Perfectly Competitive Markets]]

- Producers take prices, wages, and rents as **given**
- Due to **frictionless mobility**, both workers and land earn the same competitive return regardless of sector:

$$w_F = w_C = w \qquad r_F = r_C = r$$

- No wage or rent differentials across sectors in equilibrium (unlike the [[Specific Factors Model]], where sector-specific returns can diverge)

### 📝 Notes
>

---

## Slide 11 — Two Key Definitions

### Definition 1: [[Relative Factor Abundance]]

> **Foreign is land-abundant** relative to Home, and **Home is labor-abundant** relative to Foreign, whenever:

$$\frac{T}{L} < \frac{T^*}{L^*}$$

Home has relatively more [[Labor]] per unit of [[Land]] than Foreign.

### Definition 2: [[Relative Factor Intensity]]

> **Food is land-intensive** relative to Clothing, and **Clothing is labor-intensive** relative to Food, whenever:

$$\frac{T_F}{L_F} > \frac{T_C}{L_C}$$

This must hold for **any** possible factor prices $w$ and $r$ — i.e. **no [[factor intensity reversal]]**.

### Factor Market Clearing

$$T_F + T_C = T \qquad L_F + L_C = L$$

>

## Slide 12 — The Production Possibility Frontier

### PPF Shape in H-O

The [[Production Possibility Frontier]] is **concave** (curved), as in the [[Specific Factors Model]]:

- X-axis: $Q_C$ (Clothing)
- Y-axis: $Q_F$ (Food)

### Slope of the PPF

$$\left|\frac{dQ_F}{dQ_C}\right| = \frac{MPL_F}{MPL_C}$$

Shifting one unit of $L$ from Food to Clothing:
- Food production falls by $MPL_F$
- Clothing production rises by $MPL_C$

### Why the PPF is Concave

As $Q_C \uparrow$ (more L moves to Clothing):

$$MPL_C \downarrow, \quad MPL_F \uparrow \implies \frac{MPL_F}{MPL_C} \uparrow$$

→ [[Opportunity Cost]] of Clothing (in terms of foregone Food) **rises** → concave PPF

>

## Slide 13 & 14 — Changes in Endowment: The [[Rybczynski Theorem]]

### Theorem Statement

> **[[Rybczynski Theorem]]:** An increase in a factor endowment will **increase output** in the sector that uses that factor **intensively**, and will **decrease output** in the other sector.

### Graphical Intuition: Increase in Land (T)

- The PPF shifts **outward**, but in a **biased** way toward Food (land-intensive)
- For a given relative price $\frac{P_C}{P_F}$:
  - $Q_F \uparrow$
  - $Q_C \downarrow$

$$\Delta T \uparrow \implies \Delta Q_F \uparrow, \quad \Delta Q_C \downarrow$$

> The PPF rotates outward along the Food axis — a **biased expansion**.

>

## Slide 15 — International Trade in H-O: Which Good Does a Country Export?

### [[Heckscher-Ohlin Theorem]]

> A country has a [[Comparative Advantage]] (and therefore **exports**) the good that is **intensive in the country's relatively abundant factor**.

### Application

| Country | Abundant Factor | Exports | Imports |
|---------|----------------|---------|---------|
| **Home** | [[Labor]] (L) | Clothing (labor-intensive) | Food |
| **Foreign** | [[Land]] (T) | Food (land-intensive) | Clothing |

$$\frac{T}{L} < \frac{T^*}{L^*} \implies \text{Home exports C, Foreign exports F}$$

### 📝 Notes
>

## Slide 16 — Trade Equilibrium: Graphically

### Home (Labor-Abundant)

- PPF biased toward **Clothing** (more bowed along $Q_C$ axis)
- Autarky price: $\left(\frac{P_C}{P_F}\right)^A$ is low
- Opens to trade → specializes in **Clothing**, exports C, imports F

### Foreign (Land-Abundant)

- PPF biased toward **Food** (more bowed along $Q_F$ axis)
- Autarky price: $\left(\frac{P_F}{P_C}\right)^A$ is low
- Opens to trade → specializes in **Food**, exports F, imports C

## Slide 17 — Distributional Effects of Trade in H-O

### [[Heckscher-Ohlin Model]] — Winners and Losers

> **Owners of a country's [[abundant factor]] gain from trade; owners of the [[scarce factor]] lose.**

### Contrast with [[Specific Factors Model]]

| Dimension | [[Specific Factors Model]] | [[Heckscher-Ohlin Model]] |
|-----------|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| Time horizon | Short run | Long run |
| Factor mobility | Labor only | All factors |
| Winners | Specific factors in export sector | Owners of abundant factor |
| Losers | Specific factors in import sector | Owners of scarce factor |
| Defined by | **Current sector** | **Which factor they own** |

>

## Slide 18 & 19 — The [[Stolper-Samuelson Theorem]]

### Theorem Statement

> **[[Stolper-Samuelson Theorem]]:** An increase in the **relative price** of a good will **increase the real return** to the factor used **intensively** in the production of that good, and will **decrease the real return** to the other factor.

### Mapping to H-O Context

If $P_C \uparrow$ (clothing price rises — labor-intensive):

$$w_{\text{real}} \uparrow, \qquad r_{\text{real}} \downarrow$$

If $P_F \uparrow$ (food price rises — land-intensive):

$$r_{\text{real}} \uparrow, \qquad w_{\text{real}} \downarrow$$

### Three Key Predictions of H-O (Summary)

| Question | Theorem | Answer |
|----------|---------|--------|
| How do goods prices affect real income? | [[Stolper-Samuelson Theorem]] | Price↑ of intensive-factor good → real return of that factor↑ |
| How do factor endowments affect production? | [[Rybczynski Theorem]] | Endowment↑ → output↑ in intensive sector |
| Which good does a country export? | [[Heckscher-Ohlin Theorem]] | The good intensive in the abundant factor |

## Slide 20 — Empirical Evidence: [[Rybczynski Theorem]] — Asian Tigers

### The Asian Tigers Case

- **Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong, South Korea** — over 40 years
- Became increasingly **skilled-labor abundant** as their economies developed
- As a result, shifted exports toward **more skill-intensive goods** to the US
- Pattern: Early exports concentrated in low-skill industries; by 1998, concentrated in high-skill industries

$$\Delta \text{Skilled Labor} \uparrow \implies \Delta \text{Skill-Intensive Exports} \uparrow$$

This is the [[Rybczynski Theorem]] in action: endowment growth biases production toward intensive sectors.

>

## Slides 21–23 — Empirical Evidence: [[Stolper-Samuelson Theorem]] — Autor, Dorn & Hanson (2013)

### Paper

**Autor, Dorn, and Hanson (2013)** — *American Economic Review*

### Finding

> U.S. census zones (Commuting Zones) with **larger exposure to Chinese import competition** experienced **more unemployment and wage losses**.

### Key Results (Table 6)

Dependent variable: **10-year equivalent change in average log weekly wage**

| Sample | $\Delta$ Imports/Worker Coefficient |
|--------|--------------------------------------|
| All workers | −0.759\*\*\* |
| Males | −0.892\*\*\* |
| Females | −0.614\*\*\* |
| No college (all) | −0.814\*\*\* |

- As Chinese imports penetrated US markets, $P_{\text{manufacturing}} \downarrow$ → real wages of less-skilled (labor-intensive) workers fell
- Consistent with [[Stolper-Samuelson Theorem]]: price decline in labor-intensive sector → return to labor falls
- Geographically concentrated in the **Rust Belt** and **Southeast** manufacturing regions

## Slide 24 & 25 — Empirical Evidence: H-O Theorem — Germany vs Bangladesh

### Finding

- US imports **more** from **Germany** (skilled-labor abundant) in **skill-intensive sectors**
- US imports **more** from **Bangladesh** (unskilled-labor abundant) in **unskilled-labor-intensive sectors**
- As China upgraded its factor endowments over time (1983–2012), its export mix shifted from least-skill-intensive to more-skill-intensive sectors

$$\text{Endowment} \uparrow \text{ in skill} \implies \text{Export mix} \uparrow \text{ in skill intensity}$$

Both patterns are consistent with the [[Heckscher-Ohlin Theorem]].

## Slide 26 & 27 — The [[Leontief Paradox]]

### Setup

- First empirical test of H-O theorem: **[[Wassily Leontief]]**, 1953 (data from 1947)
- In 1947, the US was clearly **[[Capital]]-abundant** relative to the rest of the world
- H-O prediction: US should **export capital-intensive** goods and **import labor-intensive** goods

### Result (1962 Data — Table 5-2)

| | Imports | Exports |
|---|---------|---------|
| Capital per million dollars | \$2,132,000 | \$1,876,000 |
| Labor (person-years per million dollars) | 119 | 131 |
| **Capital-labor ratio** | **\$17,916** | **\$14,321** |

> US **imports were MORE capital-intensive** than its exports — the **opposite** of what H-O predicts.

$$\frac{K}{L}\bigg|_{\text{imports}} > \frac{K}{L}\bigg|_{\text{exports}} \quad \Leftarrow \textbf{ Leontief Paradox}$$

### 📝 Notes
>

---

## Slide 28 — Explaining the [[Leontief Paradox]]

### Possible Explanations

1. **Technology differences** — H-O assumes identical production technology across countries, but US and foreign technologies were not the same

2. **Ignoring [[Land]]** — Leontief only used capital and labor; the US is also [[Land]]-abundant, and land-intensive sectors (e.g. agriculture) were neither purely capital nor labor intensive

3. **Skill heterogeneity** — Leontief did not distinguish **skilled vs. unskilled [[Labor]]**; US exports may have been intensive in *human capital* (skilled labor), not physical capital

4. **Post-WWII data anomaly** — 1947 data may be unusual; World War II had ended only 2 years earlier, distorting factor use and trade patterns

5. **Trade barriers** — The US was not in completely free trade; tariffs and other trade barriers meant domestic prices differed from world prices, violating H-O assumptions

>

## Slide 29 — Saving H-O: [[Trefler (1995)]]

### The Problem

The basic H-O model assumes **identical production technology** across countries — but this is unrealistic.

### Trefler's Solution

**[[Daniel Trefler]]** (1995) introduced **country-specific technology differences** (efficiency parameters) into the H-O framework.

### Estimated Technological Efficiency (US = 1, 1983 data)

| Country | Efficiency |
|---------|-----------|
| Bangladesh | 0.03 |
| Thailand | 0.17 |
| Hong Kong | 0.40 |
| Japan | 0.70 |
| West Germany | 0.78 |
| United States | 1.00 |

- Once technology differences are accounted for, the H-O model fits trade data **substantially better**
- The [[Leontief Paradox]] largely disappears when efficiency-adjusted factor endowments are used

## Summary: The Three Theorems of H-O

$$\boxed{\text{Heckscher-Ohlin Theorem:} \quad \text{Export the good intensive in your abundant factor}}$$

$$\boxed{\text{Stolper-Samuelson:} \quad P_i \uparrow \implies \text{Real return to intensive factor} \uparrow, \text{ other factor} \downarrow}$$

$$\boxed{\text{Rybczynski:} \quad \text{Endowment}_i \uparrow \implies Q_{\text{intensive sector}} \uparrow, \; Q_{\text{other}} \downarrow}$$

## Key Equations

$$\frac{T}{L} < \frac{T^*}{L^*} \quad \text{(Home is labor-abundant)}$$

$$\frac{T_F}{L_F} > \frac{T_C}{L_C} \quad \text{(Food is land-intensive)}$$

$$\left|\frac{dQ_F}{dQ_C}\right| = \frac{MPL_F}{MPL_C} \quad \text{(PPF slope)}$$

$$w_F = w_C = w, \quad r_F = r_C = r \quad \text{(competitive equilibrium)}$$
